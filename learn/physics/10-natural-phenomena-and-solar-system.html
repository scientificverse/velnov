Topic overview

This topic joins two big stories: what causes lightning and earthquakes here on Earth, and the Sun's family of planets and the other bodies we see in the night sky.

Static charge and lightning

Rub a plastic comb on dry hair and it will pick up tiny bits of paper. Rubbing moves charge from one object to the other, so both become charged. There are two kinds of charge, positive and negative: like charges repel and unlike charges attract, the same pattern as magnetic poles. Charges that stay on a surface are called static charges. In a thunderstorm, huge charges build up in clouds. When they grow large enough, they jump to another cloud or to the ground as a giant spark: lightning. A lightning conductor, a metal rod on a tall building joined to the ground by a thick wire, carries this charge safely into the Earth. Passing charge into the Earth is called earthing.

Earthquakes

The Earth's outer layer is broken into huge slabs called tectonic plates, which move very slowly. When they suddenly slip or push against each other, the ground shakes. A seismograph records the shaking, and the Richter scale describes its strength. Indoors, take cover under a strong table, away from windows and heavy furniture; outdoors, move to open ground away from buildings and power lines.

The Sun and its family

The Sun is a star: a huge ball of hot, glowing gases that makes its own light. Its gravity keeps eight planets in orbit around it: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. Mercury is closest to the Sun, Jupiter is the largest and Saturn has the brightest rings. Mars looks red because of its iron-rich dust, and Venus is the hottest planet because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat. Earth is the only planet known to have life. The Moon, Earth's natural satellite, has no light of its own; its phases are the changing parts of its sunlit side that we see over about a month. Earth spins once a day, giving day and night, and goes round the Sun in about 365¼ days.

Words to know

Meteor
A "shooting star": a bit of rock from space glowing as it burns up in our air. One that reaches the ground is a meteorite.
Constellation
A group of stars that forms a pattern, such as Ursa Major (Saptarshi), which helps us find the Pole Star.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking rubbing creates charge. It only moves charge from one object to the other.
  • Guessing that Mercury is the hottest planet because it is closest to the Sun. Venus is hotter.
  • Calling the Sun a planet or the Moon a star. The Sun is a star; the Moon is a satellite.
  • Sheltering under a tall, lonely tree during lightning. A building or a closed car is far safer.

Q1. medium

When you rub a plastic scale (or comb) on dry hair, it can attract small bits of paper. This is because the scale becomes:

  1. A Electrically charged (static electricity)
  2. B Magnetic
  3. C Hot
  4. D Heavy

Answer: A — Electrically charged (static electricity)

Rubbing transfers charge, so the scale becomes electrically charged. This static electricity lets it attract light objects like bits of paper. Such charges produced by rubbing are called static (still) charges.

  4. D The Moon revolves around the Sun directly

Answer: A — The Sun is at the centre, and eight planets revolve around it held by gravity

The Sun is at the centre of the Solar System, and eight planets (with their moons), along with asteroids, comets and meteoroids, revolve around it, held by the Sun's gravity. The Moon revolves around the Earth, not directly around the Sun.

Which overall statement about natural phenomena is CORRECT?

  2. B Lightning is caused by magnets
  3. C Earthquakes are caused by the Moon's light
  4. D Static charge is produced by heating water

Answer: A — Static charge builds up by rubbing, lightning is a huge electric discharge, earthquakes come from moving plates, and lightning conductors and earthing keep us safe

Rubbing produces static charge; the buildup and sudden discharge of such charges in clouds causes lightning; earthquakes are caused by the movement of the Earth's tectonic plates; and safety devices like lightning conductors and earthing protect us from electrical dangers.

Which of these is a DWARF planet, no longer counted among the eight main planets?

  1. A Pluto
  2. B Mars
  3. C Neptune
  4. D Mercury

Answer: A — Pluto

Pluto was reclassified as a 'dwarf planet' in 2006 by astronomers, so it is no longer counted among the eight planets of the Solar System. Mars, Neptune and Mercury remain full planets.
