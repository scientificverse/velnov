Topic overview

Electric current can do three useful things: make a wire hot, turn a coil into a magnet, and cause chemical changes in a liquid. Almost every electrical device in your home uses at least one of these effects.

The heating effect

Every wire that carries current warms up. How much depends on the wire's material, length and thickness, and on the current. Heaters, irons and toasters use coils of nichrome, an alloy that gets very hot without melting, while the copper wires leading to them stay fairly cool. A filament bulb uses tungsten, which can glow white-hot; the glass holds an unreactive gas, or no air at all, so the filament does not burn away. A fuse uses the same effect. Its wire melts at a low temperature, so if too much current flows because of overloading or a short circuit, it melts and breaks the circuit before the house wiring can overheat.

The magnetic effect

In 1820 Hans Christian Oersted saw a compass needle move when current flowed through a nearby wire: current has a magnetic effect. Wind insulated wire into a coil round a soft-iron core and you have an electromagnet, which is a magnet only while current flows. More turns of wire or a bigger current make it stronger. Electromagnets work cranes that lift scrap iron, electric bells, loudspeakers and motors.

The chemical effect

Some liquids, such as salt water, lemon juice and tap water, conduct electricity; distilled water and oil hardly do. When current passes through a conducting liquid, it can cause chemical changes: gas bubbles at the metal rods (electrodes), a layer of metal on one rod, or a change of colour. An LED in the tester shows up even a weak current. Electroplating uses this effect to coat an object, joined to the negative terminal, with a thin layer of another metal. Chromium makes taps and bicycle parts shiny and scratch-resistant, and tin protects the iron of food cans from rusting.

Words to know

MCB
A miniature circuit breaker. Like a fuse, it cuts off a dangerous current, but it simply switches off and can be switched back on.
Galvanising
Coating iron with zinc to stop it rusting.

In everyday life

A room heater, an iron and a toaster use the heating effect; an electric bell, a crane magnet and the motor in a fan use the magnetic effect; electroplated jewellery and shiny taps show the chemical effect.

Common mistakes

  • Replacing a blown fuse with a thicker wire. It may not melt during a fault, and the wiring can catch fire.
  • Thinking an electromagnet stays magnetic after it is switched off.
  • Assuming all water conducts well. Pure distilled water is a poor conductor; the salts in tap water are what let current through.

Answer: A — The coil has high resistance and carries enough current to become very hot; ordinary wiring has low resistance

The heating element is a high-resistance wire designed to get hot (and glow) when current flows. Household copper wiring has low resistance and stays cool. Matching resistance to purpose is key in appliance design.

An electromagnet loses its magnetism when the current is switched off because:

  1. A The magnetic effect exists only while current flows through the coil
  2. B The iron core melts
  3. C The wire breaks
  4. D The core becomes a permanent magnet

Answer: A — The magnetic effect exists only while current flows through the coil

An electromagnet is magnetic only while current flows. When the current stops, the magnetic field disappears and the soft-iron core loses its magnetism. This on/off control is the electromagnet's key feature.

Q49. hard

A wire carrying current is coiled into many loops (a solenoid). Compared to a single straight wire, the coil:

  1. A Produces a much stronger magnetic field
  2. B Produces a weaker field
  3. C Produces no field
  4. D Produces only heat

Answer: A — Produces a much stronger magnetic field

Winding the wire into many loops (a solenoid) adds up the magnetic effects of each loop, producing a much stronger, more uniform magnetic field - the basis of electromagnets and electric motors.

  3. C A vacuum

Answer: A — A conducting solution, where deposits or gas bubbles appeared at the electrodes

When current passes through a conducting solution (electrolyte), chemical changes occur - such as gas bubbles forming, metal being deposited, or colour changes at the electrodes. This is the chemical effect of current.

Q53. hard

Why is a THICK fuse wire or a wrong (very high rated) fuse dangerous?

  1. A It may not melt in time during a fault, so the wiring can overheat and catch fire
  2. B It melts too quickly
  3. C It produces light
  4. D It saves electricity

Answer: A — It may not melt in time during a fault, so the wiring can overheat and catch fire

A fuse must be rated correctly. If the fuse is too thick or too high-rated, it will not melt when a dangerous current flows, so it fails to protect the circuit - the wiring can overheat and cause a fire.

  3. C It makes objects lighter

Answer: A — It gives objects an attractive finish and protects them from rust/wear using only a thin layer of costly metal

Electroplating coats a cheaper metal with a thin layer of a better metal (chromium, gold, silver, tin, zinc). This improves appearance, resists corrosion and wear, and saves money since only a thin layer of the expensive metal is used.
