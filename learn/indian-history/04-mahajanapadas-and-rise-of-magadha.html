The Mahajanapadas and the Rise of Magadha – from tribes to kingdoms

In this overview: Janapadas and Mahajanapadas · Some Mahajanapadas and their capitals · Life in the Mahajanapadas · Why Magadha became the most powerful · The dynasties of Magadha · Foreign invasions · Common misconceptions · Quick revision · Test your understanding

1. Janapadas and Mahajanapadas

A janapada is the land (pada, “foot”) where a jana (people) settled. In the 6th century BCE, 16 Mahajanapadas (“great realms”) flourished in north India.

Example: The list of 16 comes from the Buddhist text Anguttara Nikaya.

Two kinds of state

  • Monarchies (rajya): ruled by a king, e.g. Magadha, Kosala.
  • Republics (gana-sanghas): ruled by an assembly of clan chiefs, e.g. Vajji (capital Vaishali, led by the Licchavis) and Malla.

Remember: The Licchavis of Vaishali ran one of the world's earliest republics.

2. Some Mahajanapadas and their capitals

  • Magadha: Rajagriha, later Pataliputra.
  • Kosala: Shravasti.
  • Avanti: Ujjain (Magadha's chief rival).
  • Vatsa: Kaushambi.
  • Kashi: Varanasi.
  • Anga: Champa.
  • Gandhara: Taxila, a famous centre of learning.
  • Assaka: on the Godavari; the only one in the Deccan.

3. Life in the Mahajanapadas

This period is called the second urbanisation: cities grew again in the Ganga plains, more than a thousand years after the Harappan cities.

  • The first Indian coins, punch-marked coins of silver and copper, appeared.
  • The fine, glossy pottery of the age is Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW).
  • Merchants and craftsmen formed guilds called shrenis; rich merchants were called setthis.
  • The main tax was bhaga, usually one-sixth of the harvest.
  • States had standing armies and fortified capitals.

Example: Buddhism and Jainism also arose in this age, in the Magadha region.

4. Why Magadha became the most powerful

  • Fertile soil in the Ganga plains.
  • Iron ore nearby, for tools and weapons.
  • Rivers (the Ganga and Son) for trade and moving troops.
  • War elephants from the eastern forests.
  • A line of strong, ambitious rulers.

Example: Pataliputra, at the meeting of the Ganga and Son, was a natural water-fort.

5. The dynasties of Magadha

Haryanka → Shishunaga → Nanda → Maurya

A. Haryanka dynasty

  • Bimbisara: expanded by marriage alliances and by conquering Anga. He was a contemporary of the Buddha and Mahavira.
  • Ajatashatru: took the throne by killing his father. He conquered the Vajji republic, using new war engines (a catapult and a bladed chariot). He hosted the First Buddhist Council at Rajagriha.
  • Udayin: founded Pataliputra and made it the capital.

B. Shishunaga dynasty

Shishunaga destroyed the power of Avanti. His successor Kalashoka hosted the Second Buddhist Council at Vaishali.

C. Nanda dynasty

  • Mahapadma Nanda: built a huge army and empire; called the first empire builder of India.
  • Dhana Nanda: the last Nanda. He was rich but unpopular, and was overthrown by Chandragupta Maurya with Chanakya's help.

6. Foreign invasions

  • Persians: Darius I took Gandhara and the Indus region in the late 6th century BCE. This brought the Kharoshthi script.
  • Alexander the Great defeated Porus at the Battle of Hydaspes (Jhelum) in 326 BCE. His tired army refused to cross the Beas, fearing the mighty Nanda army.

Example: Alexander's campaign opened routes between India and the West, and left Greek accounts of India.
