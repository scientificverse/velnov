Topic overview

The Mauryan Empire – India's first great empire

In this overview: Founding the empire · Chandragupta Maurya · Bindusara · Ashoka the Great · Ashoka's inscriptions · Mauryan administration · Decline · Common misconceptions · Quick revision · Test your understanding

1. Founding the empire

The Mauryan Empire (c. 321–185 BCE) was the first empire to unite most of the subcontinent. Its capital was Pataliputra.

Chandragupta Maurya → Bindusara → Ashoka

2. Chandragupta Maurya

Chandragupta Maurya overthrew Dhana Nanda in c. 321 BCE, guided by Chanakya (Kautilya), who wrote the Arthashastra, a book on statecraft.

  • He defeated the Greek general Seleucus Nicator (c. 305 BCE). By the treaty he gained land in the north-west and gave 500 war elephants.
  • Seleucus sent Megasthenes as ambassador. His book Indica describes Pataliputra and Mauryan life.
  • Late in life Chandragupta became a Jain and went to Shravanabelagola with Bhadrabahu.

Example: The play Mudrarakshasa by Vishakhadatta tells how Chanakya put Chandragupta on the throne.

3. Bindusara

Bindusara, Chandragupta's son, extended the empire south. The Greeks called him Amitrochates (“slayer of foes”). He followed the Ajivika sect.

4. Ashoka the Great

Ashoka (c. 273–232 BCE) is one of the greatest rulers in world history.

The Kalinga War

In c. 261 BCE Ashoka conquered Kalinga (Odisha). The killing and suffering filled him with remorse, recorded in his 13th Major Rock Edict. He gave up war and embraced Buddhism.

Dhamma

Dhamma was Ashoka's code of moral conduct for all his people, not a single religion: tolerance of all sects, non-violence, respect for elders, kindness to servants and animals, and charity.

  • He appointed Dhamma Mahamatras to spread it.
  • He built wells, rest-houses and hospitals for people and animals, and planted shade trees along roads.
  • He sent his son Mahendra and daughter Sanghamitra to spread Buddhism in Sri Lanka.
  • He hosted the Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra.

5. Ashoka's inscriptions

Ashoka carved his messages on rocks and pillars across the empire, mostly in Prakrit, using the Brahmi script (and Kharoshthi in the north-west, with Greek and Aramaic at Kandahar).

  • There are 14 Major Rock Edicts, as well as Minor Rock Edicts and Pillar Edicts.
  • He calls himself Devanampiya Piyadasi, “Beloved of the Gods”. The Maski edict names him directly as Ashoka.
  • James Prinsep deciphered Brahmi in 1837.

Examples:

  • The Lion Capital from Sarnath is India's national emblem, and its 24-spoked wheel is the Ashoka Chakra on the national flag.
  • The pillars are famous for their mirror-like Mauryan polish, on Chunar sandstone.

6. Mauryan administration

The government was highly centralised, with the king at its head, advised by a council of ministers (Mantriparishad).

  • Provinces, under princes, had capitals at Taxila, Ujjain, Tosali and Suvarnagiri.
  • A large spy network kept the king informed.
  • Rajukas handled rural administration and justice.
  • The main tax was land revenue, one-sixth to one-fourth of the produce.
  • The state controlled mines, salt and arms.

Examples:

  • According to Megasthenes, Pataliputra was run by six boards of five members each.
  • The Sudarshana Lake at Girnar was an irrigation work built under Chandragupta.
  • The Arthashastra's Saptanga theory lists seven parts of a state: king, ministers, territory, fort, treasury, army and ally.

7. Decline

After Ashoka, the empire weakened:

  • weak successors,
  • an empire too vast to hold together,
  • a strained treasury,
  • provinces breaking away.

Example: The last Mauryan king, Brihadratha, was killed by his general Pushyamitra Shunga in c. 185 BCE, who founded the Shunga dynasty.
