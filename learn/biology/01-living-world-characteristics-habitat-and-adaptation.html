Topic overview

The Living World – what is alive, where it lives and how it survives

In this overview: Characteristics of living things · Responding to stimuli · Habitat · Adaptation · Survival behaviours · Cold-blooded and warm-blooded animals · Common misconceptions · Quick revision · Test your understanding

1. Characteristics of living things

All living things share these features:

  • Nutrition: they need food for energy, growth and repair.
  • Respiration: they release energy from food, day and night.
  • Growth: they grow from within by making new cells.
  • Excretion: they remove waste from their bodies.
  • Reproduction: they produce young of their own kind.
  • Response to stimuli: they react to changes around them.
  • Death: they have a limited lifespan.

Examples:

  • A car moves and uses fuel, but it cannot grow or reproduce, so it is not alive.
  • A crystal “grows” only by adding material on its outside; living things grow from within.

2. Responding to stimuli

A stimulus is any change in the surroundings, such as light, heat, touch or sound, that produces a reaction.

Examples:

  • You pull your hand back from a hot pan.
  • The leaves of the touch-me-not plant (Mimosa) fold when touched.
  • A plant shoot bends towards light, and roots grow down towards water and gravity.

Remember: Plants cannot walk about, but they do move parts of their bodies and respond to stimuli.

3. Habitat

A habitat is the place where an organism naturally lives. It provides food, water, air, shelter and a place to breed.

  • Terrestrial habitats are on land: forests, deserts, grasslands, mountains.
  • Aquatic habitats are in water: ponds, rivers, lakes, seas and oceans.

Biotic and abiotic components

  • Biotic components are the living things in a habitat: plants, animals and microorganisms.
  • Abiotic components are the non-living things: sunlight, air, water, soil and temperature.

4. Adaptation

An adaptation is a special feature of body or behaviour that helps an organism survive in its habitat. Adaptations develop slowly, over many generations.

A. Desert

  • Camel: long legs keep its body away from the hot sand; it passes very little urine, hardly sweats, and stores fat in its hump.
  • Cactus: leaves are reduced to spines to cut water loss; the thick green stem stores water and makes food; roots go very deep.

B. Mountains and polar regions

  • Yak, snow leopard: thick fur or a layer of fat keeps them warm.
  • Polar bear: white fur keeps it warm and hides it against the snow.
  • Mountain trees: cone-shaped, with sloping branches, so snow slides off.

C. Water

  • Fish: a streamlined body cuts water resistance; gills take in oxygen dissolved in water; fins help them swim.
  • Dolphins and whales live in water but breathe air through lungs, using a blowhole.
  • Water lily: hollow stems and broad floating leaves.
  • Frog (an amphibian): webbed feet for swimming, and lungs and strong legs for land.
  • Duck: webbed feet, and oily feathers that keep it dry.

D. Grasslands and forests

  • Deer: eyes on the sides of the head to watch for danger, and strong legs to run.
  • Lion: eyes at the front to judge distance while hunting.
  • Owl: large eyes, sharp hearing and silent flight for hunting at night.
  • Birds: hollow, light bones and strong flight muscles.

5. Survival behaviours

  • Camouflage: blending with the surroundings to hide, e.g. a chameleon changing colour.
  • Migration: travelling long distances to a better place with the seasons, e.g. the Siberian crane in winter.
  • Hibernation: a deep winter sleep to survive the cold, e.g. frogs and bears.
  • Aestivation: a summer sleep to escape heat and dryness, e.g. some snails.

6. Cold-blooded and warm-blooded animals

  • Cold-blooded animals' body temperature changes with their surroundings, e.g. snakes and lizards, which bask in the sun to warm up.
  • Warm-blooded animals keep a nearly steady body temperature, e.g. birds and mammals.

Remember: Destroying a habitat leaves its organisms nowhere to live. Most cannot simply move elsewhere, so protecting habitats protects species.

Key takeaway: Living things share the signs of life, live in habitats that meet their needs, and survive there thanks to adaptations built up over many generations.

Which of the following is a characteristic of ALL living things?

  1. A They grow, respire, reproduce and respond to stimuli
  2. B They can fly
  3. C They can move from place to place
  4. D They are green in colour

Answer: A — They grow, respire, reproduce and respond to stimuli

All living organisms need food, respire, grow, excrete, reproduce, respond to stimuli and eventually die. Not all can move about or fly - plants, for instance, stay rooted.

  1. A A stimulus
  2. B A habitat
  3. C An adaptation
  4. D Nutrition

A stimulus is any change - light, heat, touch, sound or smell - that produces a reaction. Pulling your hand back from a hot object is a response to a stimulus.

  1. A Destroying habitats leaves organisms with nowhere to live, and many species die out
  2. B Habitats have no effect on animals
  3. C Animals can live anywhere
  4. D Habitats only matter to plants

Answer: A — Destroying habitats leaves organisms with nowhere to live, and many species die out

Each organism is adapted to a particular habitat and cannot simply move elsewhere. Cutting forests and filling wetlands drives species towards extinction.

Q51. hard

Animals like snakes and lizards are cold-blooded, which means their body temperature:

  1. A Changes with the temperature of the surroundings
  2. B Always stays the same
  3. C Is always very low
  4. D Is always very high

Answer: A — Changes with the temperature of the surroundings

Cold-blooded animals cannot regulate their own body heat, so they bask in the sun to warm up and seek shade or burrows to cool down.

Which adaptation helps a snake move without legs?

  1. A A long flexible body with strong muscles and belly scales that grip the ground
  2. B Wings
  3. C Webbed feet
  4. D Hollow bones

Answer: A — A long flexible body with strong muscles and belly scales that grip the ground

A snake pushes its body into S-shaped curves. The broad belly scales catch against the surface, and waves of muscle contraction drive the body forward.

Q55. hard

Mangrove trees growing in salty coastal swamps have special roots that:

  1. A Grow upwards out of the mud to take in oxygen
  2. B Store salt only
  3. C Produce flowers
  4. D Absorb sunlight

Answer: A — Grow upwards out of the mud to take in oxygen

Waterlogged mud contains almost no oxygen, so mangroves grow breathing roots (pneumatophores) that stick up into the air. Stilt roots also anchor them against tides.

Q57. hard

Adaptations develop:

  1. A Gradually over many generations, not in a single lifetime
  2. B Instantly when an animal wants them
  3. C Only in animals
  4. D Only in plants

Answer: A — Gradually over many generations, not in a single lifetime

Individuals with helpful features survive and reproduce more successfully, so those features become common over many generations. No animal can develop an adaptation on demand.

Q60. hard

Which feature helps an owl hunt at night?

  1. A Large forward-facing eyes, sharp hearing and soft feathers for silent flight
  2. B Bright colours
  3. C Webbed feet
  4. D A long neck

Answer: A — Large forward-facing eyes, sharp hearing and soft feathers for silent flight

Big eyes gather what little light there is, asymmetrically placed ears pinpoint the smallest rustle, and fringed feathers muffle the sound of the owl's wings so prey never hears it coming.
