HCF & LCM

Product of HCF and LCM Class 6

HCF × LCM = a × b

HCFhighest common factor
LCMlowest common multiple
a, bthe two numbers

Where it is used: To find the LCM quickly when the HCF is known (or vice versa) for two numbers.

In simple words: For any two numbers, the biggest number that divides both, times the smallest number both divide into, equals the two numbers multiplied together.

Example: For 12 and 18: HCF=6, LCM=36 → 6×36 = 12×18 = 216

LCM from HCF Class 6

LCM = (a × b) ÷ HCF

a, bthe two numbers
HCFhighest common factor

Where it is used: To calculate the LCM of two numbers using their product and their HCF — used in adding fractions and solving timing problems.

In simple words: Multiply the two numbers and divide by the biggest number that divides both.

Example: a=12, b=18, HCF=6 → LCM = 216 ÷ 6 = 36

HCF and LCM of fractions

HCF = HCF(numerators)/LCM(denominators); LCM = LCM(num)/HCF(den)

numnumerators
dendenominators

Where it is used: To find the HCF or LCM of fractions.

In simple words: Special rule: swap where HCF and LCM go for the tops and bottoms.

Example: HCF of 2/3, 4/9 = HCF(2,4)/LCM(3,9) = 2/9

Exponents

Product law of exponents Class 7

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ

athe base
m, nthe powers

Where it is used: To multiply powers of the same base by adding the exponents — used all over algebra and science.

In simple words: When you multiply the same number raised to powers, just add the little numbers on top.

Example: 2³ × 2² = 2⁵ = 32

Quotient law of exponents Class 7

aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ

athe base (a ≠ 0)
m, nthe powers

Where it is used: To divide powers of the same base by subtracting the exponents.

In simple words: When you divide the same number raised to powers, subtract the little numbers on top.

Example: 2⁵ ÷ 2² = 2³ = 8

Power of a power Class 7

(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ

athe base
m, nthe powers

Where it is used: To simplify a power raised to another power by multiplying the exponents.

In simple words: A power of a power: multiply the two little top numbers together.

Example: (2³)² = 2⁶ = 64

Zero exponent Class 8

a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0)

aany non-zero number

Where it is used: A rule used constantly when simplifying algebraic and scientific expressions.

In simple words: Any number (except 0) raised to the power 0 equals 1.

Example: 7⁰ = 1, 100⁰ = 1

Negative exponent Class 8

a⁻ⁿ = 1 ÷ aⁿ

athe base (a ≠ 0)
nthe power

Where it is used: To rewrite negative powers as fractions — used in standard form and algebra.

In simple words: A negative power means 'one divided by' that power — it flips it into a fraction.

Example: 2⁻³ = 1 ÷ 2³ = 1/8

Fractional exponent (roots)

a^(1/n) = ⁿ√a

athe base
nthe root

Where it is used: To rewrite roots as powers and vice versa — used in surds, indices and calculus.

In simple words: A power of one-over-n means the nth root: power 1/2 is a square root, 1/3 is a cube root.

Example: 27^(1/3) = ³√27 = 3

Power of a power (indices)

(aᵐ)ⁿ = a^(mn), aᵐ × aⁿ = a^(m+n)

athe base
m, nthe powers

Where it is used: The core index laws used to simplify powers.

In simple words: Multiply powers of a power; add powers when multiplying the same base.

Example: (2²)³ = 2⁶ = 64

Standard Form

Standard (scientific) form Class 8

N = k × 10ⁿ, 1 ≤ k < 10

ka number between 1 and 10
nan integer power of 10

Where it is used: To write very big or very small numbers compactly — used in science for distances, sizes and masses.

In simple words: Write the number as a value between 1 and 10 times a power of ten, so huge or tiny numbers are easy to read.

Example: 300000 = 3 × 10⁵

Squares

Square of a number Class 8

Square = n × n = n²

nthe number

Where it is used: To find a number multiplied by itself — used in area, Pythagoras and algebra.

In simple words: Squaring means multiplying a number by itself.

Example: 6² = 6 × 6 = 36

Square root by the meaning

√n = m, where m × m = n

nthe number
mits square root

Where it is used: To find the number which, multiplied by itself, gives n — used in Pythagoras, areas, statistics.

In simple words: The square root asks: what number times itself makes this number?

Example: √64 = 8 because 8 × 8 = 64

Square of a number ending in 5

(n5)² = n(n+1) followed by 25

nthe digits before the 5

Where it is used: A quick trick to square any number ending in 5.

In simple words: Multiply the front part by the next number, then stick 25 on the end.

Example: 35² → 3×4=12, so 1225

Cubes

Cube of a number Class 8

Cube = n × n × n = n³

nthe number

Where it is used: To find a number multiplied by itself three times — used in volume and algebra.

In simple words: Cubing means multiplying a number by itself three times.

Example: 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64

Fractions

Adding like fractions Class 5

a/c + b/c = (a + b)/c

a, bthe numerators
cthe common denominator

Where it is used: To add fractions that share the same denominator — sharing, measuring, part-work problems.

In simple words: When the bottoms are the same, just add the tops and keep the bottom.

Example: 2/7 + 3/7 = 5/7

Multiplying fractions Class 7

a/b × c/d = (a × c)/(b × d)

a, cnumerators
b, ddenominators

Where it is used: To multiply two fractions — scaling recipes, finding a fraction of a fraction.

In simple words: Multiply the top numbers together and the bottom numbers together.

Example: 2/3 × 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2

Dividing fractions Class 7

a/b ÷ c/d = a/b × d/c

a/bfirst fraction
c/dsecond fraction (flipped)

Where it is used: To divide by a fraction by multiplying with its reciprocal (flip) — a key algebra and arithmetic skill.

In simple words: To divide by a fraction, flip the second one upside down and multiply instead.

Example: 1/2 ÷ 1/4 = 1/2 × 4/1 = 2

Subtracting like fractions

a/c − b/c = (a − b)/c

a, bnumerators
ccommon denominator

Where it is used: To subtract fractions with the same denominator.

In simple words: Same bottoms: subtract the tops and keep the bottom.

Example: 5/8 − 3/8 = 2/8 = 1/4

Adding unlike fractions

a/b + c/d = (ad + bc) ÷ bd

a/b, c/dthe two fractions

Where it is used: To add fractions with different denominators.

In simple words: Cross-multiply and add the tops over the product of the bottoms.

Example: 1/2 + 1/3 = (3+2)/6 = 5/6

Mixed number to improper fraction

a b/c = (a × c + b) ÷ c

awhole part
b/cfraction part

Where it is used: To convert a mixed number into an improper fraction before calculating.

In simple words: Multiply the whole number by the bottom, add the top, and keep the bottom.

Example: 2¾ = (2×4+3)/4 = 11/4

Divisibility

Divisibility idea (a = b × q + r) Class 6

Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder

Dividendnumber being divided
Divisornumber you divide by
Quotienthow many times it fits
Remainderwhat is left over

Where it is used: The basic relationship behind all division — used to check answers and in number theory.

In simple words: The number you start with equals how many times the divisor fits, times the divisor, plus whatever is left over.

Example: 17 = 5 × 3 + 2

Surds

Product of square roots

√a × √b = √(ab)

a, bnon-negative numbers

Where it is used: To multiply or simplify square roots (surds) in algebra.

In simple words: Multiply what's inside the roots and keep it under one root.

Example: √2 × √8 = √16 = 4

Quotient of square roots

√a ÷ √b = √(a/b)

a, bnon-negative, b ≠ 0

Where it is used: To simplify a ratio of square roots.

In simple words: Divide what's inside the roots and keep it under one root.

Example: √18 ÷ √2 = √9 = 3

Order of Operations

BODMAS rule

Brackets → Orders(powers) → Division/Multiplication → Addition/Subtraction

BODMASthe order to calculate in

Where it is used: To evaluate an expression in the correct order so everyone gets the same answer.

In simple words: Do brackets first, then powers, then × and ÷, and finally + and −.

Example: 2 + 3 × 4 = 2 + 12 = 14 (not 20)

Rational Numbers

A rational number between two numbers

Between a and b: (a + b) ÷ 2

a, bthe two numbers

Where it is used: To find a number lying between any two given numbers — shows there are infinitely many.

In simple words: The average of two numbers always sits exactly between them.

Example: Between 1/2 and 1: (1/2 + 1)/2 = 3/4

Divisibility Rules

Divisible by 2, 5 and 10

by 2: last digit even · by 5: ends in 0 or 5 · by 10: ends in 0

last digitthe units digit

Where it is used: To check quickly whether a number divides by 2, 5 or 10 without dividing.

In simple words: Just look at the last digit to test these three.

Example: 340 is divisible by 2, 5 and 10

Divisible by 3 and 9

by 3: digit sum ÷ 3 · by 9: digit sum ÷ 9

digit sumadd all the digits

Where it is used: To test divisibility by 3 or 9 using the sum of the digits.

In simple words: Add all the digits — if that total divides by 3 (or 9), so does the number.

Example: 927 → 9+2+7=18, divisible by both 3 and 9

Divisible by 4 and 8

by 4: last 2 digits ÷ 4 · by 8: last 3 digits ÷ 8

last digitsthe ending digits

Where it is used: To test divisibility by 4 or 8 using only the ending digits.

In simple words: Check the last two digits for 4, the last three for 8.

Example: 1236 → 36 ÷ 4, so divisible by 4

Divisible by 11

Difference of alternate digit sums is 0 or a multiple of 11

—odd-place sum − even-place sum

Where it is used: To test divisibility by 11.

In simple words: Add the digits in odd spots and in even spots; if the difference is 0 or 11, it divides by 11.

Example: 918082 → (9+8+8)−(1+0+2)=22, divisible by 11

Factors

Number of factors

If N = aᵖ × bᑫ × …, factors = (p+1)(q+1)…

a, bprime factors
p, qtheir powers

Where it is used: To count how many divisors a number has from its prime factorisation.

In simple words: Add one to each power in the prime factorisation and multiply them.

Example: 12 = 2²×3¹ → (2+1)(1+1) = 6 factors

Sum of factors

Sum = [(aᵖ⁺¹−1)/(a−1)] × [(bᑫ⁺¹−1)/(b−1)] …

a, bprime factors
p, qtheir powers

Where it is used: To find the total of all divisors of a number from its prime factorisation.

In simple words: A formula that adds up every divisor of a number without listing them.

Example: 12 = 2²×3 → (7)(4) = 28 (1+2+3+4+6+12)

Number Types

Types of numbers

Natural ⊂ Whole ⊂ Integers ⊂ Rational ⊂ Real

⊂is contained within

Where it is used: To classify numbers — the foundation of the number system.

In simple words: Counting numbers sit inside whole numbers, inside integers, inside fractions, inside all real numbers.

Example: −3 is an integer and a rational and a real number

Prime, composite and co-prime

Prime: exactly 2 factors · Composite: more than 2 · Co-prime: HCF = 1

factorsnumbers that divide it exactly

Where it is used: To identify prime, composite and co-prime numbers in number theory.

In simple words: A prime has only 1 and itself as factors; two co-primes share no common factor but 1.

Example: 7 is prime; 8 is composite; 8 and 9 are co-prime

Decimals

Recurring decimal to fraction

0.ā = a/9, 0.āb̄ = ab/99

a, abthe repeating digits

Where it is used: To turn a repeating decimal into an exact fraction.

In simple words: Put the repeating digits over as many 9s as there are repeating digits.

Example: 0.333… = 3/9 = 1/3

Unit Digit

Unit digit of a power (cyclicity)

Unit digits repeat in cycles (2,4,8,6…), period 4

—the last digit of the base

Where it is used: To find the last digit of a large power without full calculation.

In simple words: The last digit of powers repeats in a short cycle, so you only need the remainder of the power ÷ 4.

Example: 2¹⁰ ends in 4 (cycle 2,4,8,6)

Place Value

Place value vs face value

Place value = digit × its place; Face value = the digit itself

digita single figure in the number

Where it is used: To distinguish a digit's position-based value from the digit itself.

In simple words: Face value is the digit as-is; place value depends on where it sits.

Example: In 57, place value of 5 is 50, face value is 5

Consecutive Numbers

Sum of consecutive integers

Sum = (number of terms) × (average of first and last)

average(first + last) ÷ 2

Where it is used: To add a run of consecutive numbers quickly.

In simple words: Multiply how many numbers there are by the average of the ends.

Example: 5+6+7+8 = 4 × 6.5 = 26

Comparing

Comparing fractions (cross multiply)

a/b vs c/d: compare a×d with b×c

a/b, c/dthe two fractions

Where it is used: To decide which of two fractions is larger without a common denominator.

In simple words: Cross-multiply; the bigger product sits above the bigger fraction.
