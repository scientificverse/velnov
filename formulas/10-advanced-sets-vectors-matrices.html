Sets

Number of elements in a union Class 11

n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)

n(A), n(B)sizes of sets A and B
n(A ∩ B)number common to both

Where it is used: To count elements in the union of two overlapping sets — surveys, group counting without double-counting.

In simple words: Add the two group sizes, then subtract the overlap so shared members aren't counted twice.

Example: n(A)=20, n(B)=15, common 5 → 20+15−5 = 30

Union of three sets

n(A∪B∪C) = n(A)+n(B)+n(C) − n(A∩B) − n(B∩C) − n(C∩A) + n(A∩B∩C)

n(...)sizes of the sets and overlaps

Where it is used: To count elements in three overlapping sets — survey problems with three categories.

In simple words: Add the three groups, remove each double-counted overlap, then add back the triple overlap.

Example: Used in 'students who like tea/coffee/juice' problems

Number of subsets

Subsets = 2ⁿ, Proper subsets = 2ⁿ − 1

nnumber of elements in the set

Where it is used: To count how many subsets a set has.

In simple words: Each element is either in or out, giving 2 choices each, so 2ⁿ subsets in all.

Example: Set {a,b,c} has 2³ = 8 subsets

De Morgan's laws

(A∪B)' = A'∩B', (A∩B)' = A'∪B'

'complement (not in the set)

Where it is used: To simplify complements of unions and intersections — logic and set theory.

In simple words: The 'not' of an OR becomes an AND of 'nots', and vice versa.

Example: Not(A or B) = (not A) and (not B)

Permutations

Permutations (arrangements) Class 11

ⁿPᵣ = n! ÷ (n − r)!

ntotal items
ritems chosen and arranged
!factorial

Where it is used: To count ordered arrangements — passwords, seat orders, race finishes where order matters.

In simple words: Counts how many ways to arrange r things out of n when the order matters.

Example: ⁵P₂ = 5!/3! = 20

Factorial

n! = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × … × 2 × 1

na whole number
0!defined as 1

Where it is used: The building block of permutations and combinations — counting arrangements.

In simple words: Multiply all whole numbers from n down to 1. It counts how many ways to order n things.

Example: 5! = 5×4×3×2×1 = 120

Circular permutations

Circular arrangements = (n − 1)!

nnumber of items in a circle

Where it is used: To count seatings around a round table, where rotations are the same.

In simple words: In a circle there's no fixed start, so fix one person and arrange the rest: (n−1)!.

Example: 5 people round a table → 4! = 24 ways

Permutations with repeated items

Arrangements = n! ÷ (p! × q! × …)

ntotal items
p, qcounts of each repeated item

Where it is used: To count arrangements of a word or set with repeated letters.

In simple words: Divide by the factorials of the repeats, since swapping identical items doesn't make a new order.

Example: LEVEL: 5!/(2!·2!) = 30 arrangements

Combinations

Combinations (selections) Class 11

ⁿCᵣ = n! ÷ [r!(n − r)!]

ntotal items
ritems chosen
!factorial

Where it is used: To count selections where order does NOT matter — teams, committees, lottery picks.

In simple words: Counts how many ways to pick r things out of n when the order doesn't matter.

Example: ⁵C₂ = 5!/(2!·3!) = 10

Relation between nPr and nCr

ⁿPᵣ = ⁿCᵣ × r!

ⁿPᵣarrangements
ⁿCᵣselections
r!r factorial

Where it is used: To convert between selections and arrangements of r items from n.

In simple words: Arrangements = selections times the number of ways to order the chosen r items.

Example: ⁵P₂ = ⁵C₂ × 2! = 10 × 2 = 20

Symmetry of combinations

ⁿCᵣ = ⁿCₙ₋ᵣ, ⁿC₀ + ⁿC₁ + … + ⁿCₙ = 2ⁿ

n, rtotal and chosen

Where it is used: Useful identities for combinations — choosing r is the same as leaving out (n−r).

In simple words: Picking r to keep is the same as picking (n−r) to drop; all the combinations add to 2ⁿ.

Example: ⁵C₂ = ⁵C₃ = 10

Pascal's rule

ⁿCᵣ + ⁿCᵣ₋₁ = ⁿ⁺¹Cᵣ

n, rtotal and chosen

Where it is used: The rule that builds Pascal's triangle and the binomial coefficients.

In simple words: Each number in Pascal's triangle is the sum of the two above it.

Example: ⁴C₂ + ⁴C₁ = 6 + 4 = 10 = ⁵C₂

Binomial Theorem

General term of a binomial expansion Class 11

Tᵣ₊₁ = ⁿCᵣ · aⁿ⁻ʳ · bʳ

nthe power
rterm position (from 0)
a, bthe two terms of (a+b)ⁿ

Where it is used: To find any single term in the expansion of (a+b)ⁿ without expanding the whole thing.

In simple words: A shortcut to jump straight to the term you want when expanding a bracket raised to a power.

Example: (x+2)⁵, term with x³: ⁵C₂·x³·2² = 40x³

Binomial expansion

(a + b)ⁿ = Σ ⁿCᵣ · aⁿ⁻ʳ · bʳ

nthe power
ⁿCᵣthe binomial coefficients

Where it is used: To expand a bracket raised to a whole-number power without multiplying it out step by step.

In simple words: A shortcut to expand (a+b) raised to any power using the counting numbers ⁿCᵣ.

Example: (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b²

Number of terms and middle term

Terms in (a+b)ⁿ = n+1; middle term at (n/2 + 1) if n even

nthe power

Where it is used: To locate the middle term of a binomial expansion.

In simple words: An expansion of power n has n+1 terms; the middle one is often asked for.

Example: (a+b)⁶ has 7 terms; middle is the 4th

Sum of binomial coefficients

ⁿC₀ + ⁿC₁ + … + ⁿCₙ = 2ⁿ

nthe power

Where it is used: To find the sum of all coefficients in a binomial expansion (put a=b=1).

In simple words: Setting both terms to 1 shows all the coefficients add to 2ⁿ.

Example: (1+1)³ = 8 = 1+3+3+1

Complex Numbers

Modulus of a complex number Class 11

|z| = √(a² + b²), z = a + bi

areal part
bimaginary part
i√(−1)

Where it is used: To find the size (distance from origin) of a complex number — used in engineering and physics.

In simple words: Treat the real and imaginary parts like the sides of a right triangle; the modulus is the hypotenuse.

Example: z = 3 + 4i → |z| = √(9+16) = 5

Powers of i

i² = −1, i³ = −i, i⁴ = 1 (i = √−1)

ithe imaginary unit √(−1)

Where it is used: The basic rules for simplifying powers of i in complex-number arithmetic.

In simple words: i squared is −1, and higher powers cycle in a pattern of four.

Example: i⁴ = 1, so i⁵ = i

Conjugate of a complex number

z = a + bi → z̄ = a − bi

areal part
bimaginary part

Where it is used: Used to divide complex numbers and to find modulus (z·z̄ = |z|²).

In simple words: The conjugate just flips the sign of the imaginary part.

Example: z = 3+4i → z̄ = 3−4i

Product of modulus

|z₁ z₂| = |z₁| × |z₂|

z₁, z₂two complex numbers

Where it is used: To find the modulus of a product of complex numbers.

In simple words: The size of a product equals the product of the sizes.

Example: |z₁|=2, |z₂|=3 → |z₁z₂| = 6

Matrices

Order of a matrix Class 12

Order = (rows) × (columns) = m × n

mnumber of rows
nnumber of columns

Where it is used: To describe the size of a matrix, which decides whether operations like multiplication are possible.

In simple words: Count the rows and columns; a matrix with m rows and n columns is 'm by n'.

Example: A matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns is order 2×3

Identity matrix property

A × I = I × A = A

Aany square matrix
Ithe identity matrix

Where it is used: The identity matrix acts like the number 1 for matrices — used in inverses and solving systems.

In simple words: Just as multiplying a number by 1 leaves it unchanged, multiplying a matrix by I leaves it unchanged.

Example: Any 2×2 matrix times the 2×2 identity stays the same

Determinants

Determinant of a 2×2 matrix Class 12

|A| = ad − bc, A = [[a, b], [c, d]]

a, b, c, dthe four entries of the matrix

Where it is used: To solve linear equations, find inverses and check if a matrix is invertible.

In simple words: Multiply the main diagonal, subtract the other diagonal's product.

Example: [[1,2],[3,4]] → 1·4 − 2·3 = −2

Area of triangle (determinant)

Area = ½ |det of the coordinate matrix|

detthe 3×3 determinant of the vertices

Where it is used: To find a triangle's area from coordinates using a determinant (Class 12).

In simple words: A neat determinant of the three corner points gives twice the area.

Example: Vertices (0,0),(4,0),(0,3) → area 6

Determinant of a 3×3 matrix

|A| = a(ei − fh) − b(di − fg) + c(dh − eg)

a…ithe nine entries by rows

Where it is used: To evaluate a 3×3 determinant by expansion along the first row.

In simple words: Expand along the top row, alternating signs, using 2×2 determinants.

Example: Used to solve 3-variable equations

Vectors

Magnitude of a vector Class 12

|v| = √(x² + y² + z²)

x, y, zthe vector's components

Where it is used: To find the length of a vector in 2D or 3D — physics (force, velocity), computer graphics.

In simple words: Square each direction component, add them, and take the square root to get the arrow's length.

Example: v = (2,3,6) → |v| = √(4+9+36) = 7

Dot product of two vectors Class 12

a · b = |a||b| cos θ

|a|, |b|the vectors' magnitudes
θangle between them

Where it is used: To find the angle between vectors or the work done by a force — physics and geometry.

In simple words: Multiplies two arrows in a way that depends on how much they point the same way.

Example: If a⊥b (θ=90°), a·b = 0

Cross product magnitude

|a × b| = |a||b| sin θ

|a|,|b|the magnitudes
θangle between them

Where it is used: To find the area of a parallelogram formed by two vectors, and directions in physics (torque, force).

In simple words: Multiplies two arrows in a way that is largest when they point at right angles.

Example: If a∥b (θ=0), a × b = 0

Unit vector

â = a⃗ ÷ |a⃗|

a⃗the vector
|a⃗|its magnitude

Where it is used: To find a vector of length 1 in the same direction — directions in physics and graphics.

In simple words: Divide a vector by its own length to get a pure-direction arrow of length 1.

Example: a⃗=(3,4), |a⃗|=5 → â = (0.6, 0.8)

Angle between two vectors

cos θ = (a⃗ · b⃗) ÷ (|a⃗| |b⃗|)

a⃗ · b⃗the dot product
|a⃗|,|b⃗|magnitudes

Where it is used: To find the angle between two vectors — forces, directions, geometry.

In simple words: The dot product over the two lengths gives the cosine of the angle between them.

Example: Perpendicular vectors → cosθ = 0, θ = 90°

Functions

y = f(x)

xthe input
fthe rule
ythe output

Where it is used: The basic idea of a function — a rule that turns each input into exactly one output. Foundation of all higher maths.

In simple words: A function is a machine: put a number in, apply the rule, and get one number out.

Example: f(x) = 2x+1 → f(3) = 7
