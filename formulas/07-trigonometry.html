Ratios

Sine ratio Class 10

sin θ = Opposite ÷ Hypotenuse

θthe angle
Oppositeside facing the angle
Hypotenuselongest side

Where it is used: In a right triangle, to link an angle to two of its sides — heights, distances, physics, waves.

In simple words: For a given corner angle, sine is the side across from it divided by the longest slanting side.

Example: Opposite=3, Hyp=5 → sin θ = 3/5 = 0.6

Cosine ratio Class 10

cos θ = Adjacent ÷ Hypotenuse

θthe angle
Adjacentside next to the angle
Hypotenuselongest side

Where it is used: To relate an angle to the side beside it and the hypotenuse — surveying, navigation, forces.

In simple words: Cosine is the side next to the angle divided by the longest slanting side.

Example: Adjacent=4, Hyp=5 → cos θ = 4/5 = 0.8

Tangent ratio Class 10

tan θ = Opposite ÷ Adjacent

θthe angle
Oppositeside facing the angle
Adjacentside next to the angle

Where it is used: To find an angle or height from the two shorter sides — ramps, slopes, heights of buildings.

In simple words: Tangent is the side across from the angle divided by the side next to it. Also sin ÷ cos.

Example: Opposite=3, Adjacent=4 → tan θ = 3/4 = 0.75

The six ratios (SOH-CAH-TOA)

sin=O/H, cos=A/H, tan=O/A, cosec=H/O, sec=H/A, cot=A/O

Oopposite side
Aadjacent side
Hhypotenuse

Where it is used: The complete set of the six trig ratios in a right triangle, the base of all trigonometry.

In simple words: 'SOH-CAH-TOA' helps you remember the first three; the last three are just their flips.

Example: O=3, A=4, H=5 → sin=3/5, cos=4/5, tan=3/4

Complementary reciprocal ratios

sec(90°−θ)=cosecθ, cosec(90°−θ)=secθ

θthe angle

Where it is used: Cofunction identities for the reciprocal ratios.

In simple words: Secant of an angle equals cosecant of its complement, and vice versa.

Example: sec60° = cosec30° = 2

Value ranges of sin and cos

−1 ≤ sinθ ≤ 1, −1 ≤ cosθ ≤ 1

θany angle

Where it is used: To check that a sine or cosine answer is valid — they never leave [−1, 1].

In simple words: Sine and cosine always stay between −1 and 1, whatever the angle.

Example: sinθ = 2 has no solution

Identities

Pythagorean identity Class 10

sin²θ + cos²θ = 1

θany angle

Where it is used: The most-used trig identity — to find one ratio from another and to simplify expressions.

In simple words: For any angle, the square of its sine plus the square of its cosine is always exactly 1.

Example: sin30°=0.5, cos30°≈0.866 → 0.25+0.75 = 1

Tangent identity Class 10

tan θ = sin θ ÷ cos θ

θthe angle (cos θ ≠ 0)

Where it is used: To rewrite tangent in terms of sine and cosine when simplifying or solving.

In simple words: Tangent is just sine divided by cosine for the same angle.

Example: tan45° = sin45°/cos45° = 1

Sine of a sum Class 11

sin(A + B) = sinA cosB + cosA sinB

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: To expand the sine of a combined angle — used in waves, oscillations and advanced trig.

In simple words: Breaks the sine of two added angles into a combination of their sines and cosines.

Example: sin(30°+60°) = sin90° = 1

Cosine of a sum Class 11

cos(A + B) = cosA cosB − sinA sinB

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: To expand the cosine of a combined angle in trigonometry and physics.

In simple words: Breaks the cosine of two added angles into their cosines and sines combined.

Example: cos(30°+60°) = cos90° = 0

Secant identity

1 + tan²θ = sec²θ

θany angle

Where it is used: A key identity used to switch between tangent and secant when simplifying or in calculus.

In simple words: One plus tangent-squared always equals secant-squared for the same angle.

Example: tan45°=1 → 1+1 = 2 = sec²45°

Cosecant identity

1 + cot²θ = cosec²θ

θany angle

Where it is used: Used to switch between cotangent and cosecant in proofs and integration.

In simple words: One plus cotangent-squared always equals cosecant-squared for the same angle.

Example: cot45°=1 → 1+1 = 2 = cosec²45°

cos2A in terms of tanA

cos2A = (1 − tan²A) ÷ (1 + tan²A)

Athe angle

Where it is used: A tangent form of the double-angle cosine — used in integration substitution.

In simple words: Gives cosine of double the angle using only the tangent of the single angle.

Example: A=45°, tanA=1 → cos90° = 0

sin2A in terms of tanA

sin2A = 2tanA ÷ (1 + tan²A)

Athe angle

Where it is used: A tangent form of the double-angle sine — used in integration substitution.

In simple words: Gives sine of double the angle using only the tangent of the single angle.

Example: A=45°, tanA=1 → sin90° = 1

Complementary product identity

tanθ × cotθ = 1, sinθ × cosecθ = 1, cosθ × secθ = 1

θany angle

Where it is used: The reciprocal pairs multiply to 1 — used to simplify expressions.

In simple words: Each ratio times its flip equals 1.

Example: tan30° × cot30° = 1

Standard Values

Standard angle values (sin) Class 10

sin0°=0, sin30°=½, sin45°=1/√2, sin60°=√3/2, sin90°=1

θcommon angles 0°–90°

Where it is used: The key values memorised for quick trig calculations in exams and problems.

In simple words: These are the ready answers for sine of the common angles, no calculator needed.

Example: sin30° = ½ = 0.5

Standard angle values (cos)

cos0°=1, cos30°=√3/2, cos45°=1/√2, cos60°=½, cos90°=0

θcommon angles 0°–90°

Where it is used: The memorised cosine values for quick trig calculations in exams.

In simple words: Ready answers for cosine of the common angles — notice they run opposite to the sine values.

Example: cos60° = ½ = 0.5

Standard angle values (tan)

tan0°=0, tan30°=1/√3, tan45°=1, tan60°=√3, tan90°=∞

θcommon angles 0°–90°

Where it is used: The memorised tangent values, used in heights-and-distances and geometry problems.

In simple words: Ready answers for tangent of the common angles; tan45° = 1 is the easiest to remember.

Example: tan60° = √3 ≈ 1.732

Standard values (cosec, sec, cot)

cosec30°=2, sec60°=2, cot45°=1, cosec45°=√2, sec45°=√2

θcommon angles

Where it is used: The reciprocal-ratio values for the standard angles, for quick calculation.

In simple words: Just the flips of the sine, cosine and tangent standard values.

Example: cosec30° = 1/sin30° = 1/0.5 = 2

Quadrantal angle values

sin0°=0, sin90°=1, sin180°=0, sin270°=−1

0°,90°,180°,270°the quadrant boundaries

Where it is used: The sine values at the four main compass directions of the circle.

In simple words: Sine's values at the top, bottom and sides of the circle.

Example: sin270° = −1 (bottom of the circle)

Reciprocal Ratios

Cosecant, secant, cotangent Class 10

cosec θ = 1/sin θ, sec θ = 1/cos θ, cot θ = 1/tan θ

θthe angle

Where it is used: The reciprocal trig ratios, used in identities, calculus and advanced problems.

In simple words: These are just 'one divided by' the three main ratios — the flipped versions.

Example: sin30°=0.5 → cosec30° = 1/0.5 = 2

Cotangent as cos over sin

cot θ = cos θ ÷ sin θ

θthe angle (sin θ ≠ 0)

Where it is used: To rewrite cotangent using sine and cosine when simplifying trig expressions.

In simple words: Cotangent is cosine divided by sine — the upside-down of tangent.

Example: cot45° = cos45°/sin45° = 1

Heights & Distances

Height using angle of elevation Class 10

Height = Distance × tan θ

θangle of elevation
Distancehorizontal distance to the object

Where it is used: To find the height of a tower, tree or building from a distance and the angle looking up.

In simple words: Stand back a known distance, measure the up-angle to the top, and this gives the height.

Example: Distance=30 m, θ=45° → Height = 30 × 1 = 30 m

Distance from angle of elevation

Distance = Height ÷ tan θ

θangle of elevation
Heightheight of the object

Where it is used: To find how far you are standing from a tower or tree from its height and the up-angle.

In simple words: Knowing the height and the angle you look up at, this gives how far away you are.

Example: Height=30 m, θ=45° → Distance = 30 ÷ 1 = 30 m

Angle of depression

Angle of depression = Angle of elevation (alternate angles)

—measured downward from the horizontal

Where it is used: To solve problems where you look DOWN at an object from a height.

In simple words: The down-angle from the top equals the up-angle from the bottom, so the same maths applies.

Example: From a cliff, the depression to a boat equals the boat's elevation to the cliff top.

Compound Angles

Sine of a difference

sin(A − B) = sinA cosB − cosA sinB

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: To expand the sine of a difference of angles — trig proofs, waves, physics.

In simple words: Like the sum version, but with a minus in the middle.

Example: sin(60°−30°) = sin30° = 0.5

Cosine of a difference

cos(A − B) = cosA cosB + sinA sinB

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: To expand the cosine of a difference of angles in trigonometry.

In simple words: Like the cosine sum version, but with a plus in the middle.

Example: cos(60°−30°) = cos30° ≈ 0.866

Tangent of a sum

tan(A + B) = (tanA + tanB) ÷ (1 − tanA tanB)

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: To find the tangent of a combined angle — used in slopes and rotations.

In simple words: Combines the two tangents on top, with a correction underneath.

Example: tan(45°+45°) = tan90° = ∞ (denominator 0)

Tangent of a difference

tan(A − B) = (tanA − tanB) ÷ (1 + tanA tanB)

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: To find the tangent of a difference of angles — slopes, angle between lines.

In simple words: Like the tangent sum, but signs swap: minus on top, plus underneath.

Example: tan(45°−45°) = tan0° = 0

Double Angle

Sine of double angle

sin 2A = 2 sinA cosA

Athe angle

Where it is used: To rewrite sin of twice an angle — used in waves, integration and simplification.

In simple words: Sine of double the angle is twice the sine times the cosine of the single angle.

Example: sin60° = 2·sin30°·cos30° = 2·½·(√3/2) = √3/2

Cosine of double angle

cos 2A = cos²A − sin²A = 1 − 2sin²A = 2cos²A − 1

Athe angle

Where it is used: Three useful forms for cos of a double angle — chosen to suit the problem.

In simple words: Different ways to write cosine of double an angle; pick whichever fits what you already know.

Example: cos60° = 1 − 2sin²30° = 1 − 2(¼) = ½

Tangent of double angle

tan 2A = 2 tanA ÷ (1 − tan²A)

Athe angle

Where it is used: To find the tangent of twice an angle in trig and calculus.

In simple words: A ready formula for tangent of double an angle from the single-angle tangent.

Example: A=30°, tan30°=1/√3 → tan60° = (2/√3)/(1−1/3) = √3

sinθ and cosθ using tan(θ/2)

sinθ = 2t/(1+t²), cosθ = (1−t²)/(1+t²), t = tan(θ/2)

ttan of the half angle

Where it is used: To express sine and cosine through a single variable — used in integration substitutions.

In simple words: Everything can be written in terms of the tangent of half the angle.

Example: θ=90°, t=1 → sin = 2/2 = 1, cos = 0

Laws (Sine & Cosine)

Sine rule

a/sinA = b/sinB = c/sinC

a, b, cthe three sides
A, B, Cthe angles opposite them

Where it is used: To find unknown sides or angles in ANY triangle (not just right-angled) — surveying, navigation.

In simple words: In any triangle, each side divided by the sine of its opposite angle gives the same value.

Example: If a=7, A=30°, B=45° → b = 7·sin45°/sin30°

Cosine rule

c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cosC

a, b, cthe three sides
Cangle opposite side c

Where it is used: To find the third side of any triangle from two sides and the angle between them, or to find an angle from three sides.

In simple words: A stronger Pythagoras that works for any triangle, adjusting for the angle between the sides.

Example: a=3, b=4, C=90° → c² = 9+16−0 = 25, c=5

Area of a triangle (two sides & angle)

Area = ½ × a × b × sinC

a, btwo sides
Cthe angle between them

Where it is used: To find a triangle's area from two sides and the included angle — surveying, geometry.

In simple words: Half the product of two sides times the sine of the angle between them.

Example: a=6, b=8, C=30° → ½·6·8·0.5 = 12

Projection formula

a = b·cosC + c·cosB

a, b, csides
B, Cangles opposite b and c

Where it is used: Expresses one side of a triangle as the sum of projections of the other two — used in triangle proofs.

In simple words: One side equals the shadows the other two sides cast along it.

Example: Holds for any triangle's sides and angles.

Cosine rule for an angle

cosC = (a² + b² − c²) ÷ 2ab

a, b, cthe three sides
Cangle opposite side c

Where it is used: To find an angle of a triangle when all three sides are known.

In simple words: Rearranges the cosine rule to give an angle from the three side lengths.

Example: a=b=c → cosC = 1/2, so C = 60° (equilateral)

Radians

Degrees to radians

Radians = Degrees × (π ÷ 180)

πpi ≈ 3.14159
180degrees in π radians

Where it is used: To convert an angle from degrees to radians — needed in higher maths, calculus and physics.

In simple words: Multiply the degrees by π and divide by 180 to get the same angle in radians.

Example: 90° = 90 × π/180 = π/2 radians

Arc length in radians

s = r × θ

sarc length
rradius
θangle in radians

Where it is used: To find the length of a circular arc when the angle is in radians — physics, engineering.

In simple words: Multiply the radius by the angle (in radians) to get the arc length.

Example: r=5, θ=2 rad → s = 10 units

Radians to degrees

Degrees = Radians × (180 ÷ π)

πpi ≈ 3.14159

Where it is used: To convert an angle from radians back to degrees.

In simple words: Multiply radians by 180 and divide by π to get degrees.

Example: π/2 rad = 90°

Quadrants & Signs

Sign of ratios (ASTC rule)

Q1: All +, Q2: Sin +, Q3: Tan +, Q4: Cos +

Q1–Q4the four quadrants (0–90, 90–180, 180–270, 270–360)

Where it is used: To decide whether a trig ratio is positive or negative for angles beyond 90°.

In simple words: 'All-Silver-Tea-Cups': tells which ratios stay positive in each quarter turn.

Example: cos300° is positive (Q4), sin200° is negative (Q3)

Allied Angles

Negative angle (even–odd) identities

sin(−θ)=−sinθ, cos(−θ)=cosθ, tan(−θ)=−tanθ

θthe angle

Where it is used: To simplify trig of negative angles — cosine is even, sine and tangent are odd.

In simple words: A minus angle keeps cosine the same but flips the sign of sine and tangent.

Example: sin(−30°) = −sin30° = −0.5

Cofunction identities (90° − θ)

sin(90°−θ)=cosθ, cos(90°−θ)=sinθ, tan(90°−θ)=cotθ

θthe angle

Where it is used: To convert between sine/cosine and tangent/cotangent of complementary angles.

In simple words: The sine of an angle equals the cosine of its complement, and vice versa.

Example: sin60° = cos30°

Supplementary angle (180° − θ)

sin(180°−θ)=sinθ, cos(180°−θ)=−cosθ, tan(180°−θ)=−tanθ

θthe angle

Where it is used: To find trig ratios of second-quadrant angles from first-quadrant ones.

In simple words: For an angle and its supplement, sine stays the same but cosine and tangent flip sign.

Example: sin150° = sin30° = 0.5

Angles of the form (90° + θ)

sin(90°+θ)=cosθ, cos(90°+θ)=−sinθ, tan(90°+θ)=−cotθ

θthe angle

Where it is used: To reduce second-quadrant angles to first-quadrant ratios.

In simple words: Adding 90° swaps sine and cosine and adjusts the sign by quadrant.

Example: sin120° = sin(90°+30°) = cos30° ≈ 0.866

Reflex angles (360° − θ)

sin(360°−θ)=−sinθ, cos(360°−θ)=cosθ, tan(360°−θ)=−tanθ

θthe angle

Where it is used: To find ratios of fourth-quadrant angles from first-quadrant ones.

In simple words: An angle just short of a full turn keeps cosine but flips sine and tangent.

Example: cos330° = cos30° ≈ 0.866

Periodicity

Periodic identities

sin(θ+360°)=sinθ, cos(θ+360°)=cosθ, tan(θ+180°)=tanθ

θthe angle

Where it is used: To reduce very large angles by removing whole turns before finding the ratio.

In simple words: Trig ratios repeat every full turn (360°); tangent repeats every half turn.

Example: cos400° = cos40°

Triple Angle

Triple angle formulas

sin3A = 3sinA − 4sin³A, cos3A = 4cos³A − 3cosA

Athe angle

Where it is used: To expand sine and cosine of three times an angle — used in equations and proofs.

In simple words: Ready formulas for the sine and cosine of triple an angle.

Example: sin90° = 3sin30° − 4sin³30° = 1.5 − 0.5 = 1

Tangent of triple angle

tan3A = (3tanA − tan³A) ÷ (1 − 3tan²A)

Athe angle

Where it is used: To expand the tangent of three times an angle.

In simple words: A ready formula for tangent of triple an angle from the single-angle tangent.

Example: Used to solve tan-based triple-angle equations.

Product to Sum

Product-to-sum formulas

2sinA cosB = sin(A+B) + sin(A−B)

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: To turn a product of ratios into a sum — used in integration and wave problems.

In simple words: Rewrites a multiplication of sine and cosine as an addition, which is easier to handle.

Example: 2sin45°cos15° = sin60° + sin30°

2 cosA cosB and 2 sinA sinB

2cosA cosB = cos(A−B)+cos(A+B), 2sinA sinB = cos(A−B)−cos(A+B)

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: To turn products of two cosines or two sines into sums — used in integration.

In simple words: Rewrites a product of two cosines (or two sines) as a sum of cosines.

Example: 2cos45°cos15° = cos30° + cos60°

2 cosA sinB

2cosA sinB = sin(A+B) − sin(A−B)

A, Btwo angles

Where it is used: The fourth product-to-sum identity, completing the set.

In simple words: Turns cosine times sine into a difference of sines.

Example: 2cos60°sin30° = sin90° − sin30°

Sum to Product

Sum-to-product formulas

sinC + sinD = 2 sin((C+D)/2) cos((C−D)/2)

C, Dtwo angles

Where it is used: To turn a sum of sines into a product — used in solving trig equations.

In simple words: Rewrites the sum of two sines as a neat product of a sine and a cosine.

Example: sin50° + sin10° = 2 sin30° cos20°

Difference of sines

sinC − sinD = 2 cos((C+D)/2) sin((C−D)/2)

C, Dtwo angles

Where it is used: To turn a difference of sines into a product — solving trig equations.

In simple words: Rewrites one sine minus another as a neat product.

Example: sin50° − sin10° = 2 cos30° sin20°

Sum and difference of cosines

cosC+cosD = 2cos((C+D)/2)cos((C−D)/2); cosC−cosD = −2sin(..)sin(..)

C, Dtwo angles

Where it is used: To turn a sum or difference of cosines into a product.

In simple words: Rewrites two cosines added or subtracted as a product form.

Example: cos40° + cos20° = 2 cos30° cos10°

Inverse Trigonometry

Complementary inverse identities

sin⁻¹x + cos⁻¹x = 90°, tan⁻¹x + cot⁻¹x = 90°

xthe value (−1 ≤ x ≤ 1 for sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹)

Where it is used: Standard inverse-trig identities used in Class 12 calculus and equations.

In simple words: The inverse-sine and inverse-cosine of the same number always add up to 90°.

Example: sin⁻¹(0.5)=30°, cos⁻¹(0.5)=60° → 90°

Sum of two inverse tangents

tan⁻¹x + tan⁻¹y = tan⁻¹((x+y)/(1−xy)), xy < 1

x, ythe two values

Where it is used: To combine two inverse tangents into one — used in Class 12 problems.

In simple words: Adds two inverse-tangent angles into a single inverse tangent.

Example: tan⁻¹1 + tan⁻¹(1/2) combine via this rule

Principal value ranges

sin⁻¹: [−90°,90°], cos⁻¹: [0°,180°], tan⁻¹: (−90°,90°)

—the allowed output angles

Where it is used: The standard output ranges that make inverse trig single-valued (Class 12).

In simple words: Each inverse trig gives an angle only within a fixed range, so the answer is unique.

Example: sin⁻¹(0.5) = 30°, not 150°

Double of inverse tangent

2 tan⁻¹x = tan⁻¹(2x/(1−x²)), |x| < 1

xthe value

Where it is used: To simplify twice an inverse tangent into a single inverse tangent.

In simple words: Combines two equal inverse-tangent angles into one.

Example: 2tan⁻¹(1/3) = tan⁻¹(3/4)

Maxima & Minima

Range of a·sinθ + b·cosθ

max = √(a²+b²), min = −√(a²+b²)

a, bthe coefficients

Where it is used: To find the largest and smallest values of a sine-plus-cosine expression — used in optimisation and waves.

In simple words: A sine and cosine added together swings between plus and minus the root of a²+b².

Example: 3sinθ+4cosθ ranges from −5 to 5

Max and min of sinθ·cosθ

sinθ cosθ = ½ sin2θ, so max = ½, min = −½

θthe angle

Where it is used: To find the largest value of a sine-times-cosine product — optimisation.

In simple words: Sine times cosine is just half of sin(2θ), so it never exceeds one-half.

Example: Greatest value of sinθcosθ is ½ (at 45°)

Half Angle

Half-angle in terms of cos A

sin²(A/2) = (1−cosA)/2, cos²(A/2) = (1+cosA)/2

Athe angle

Where it is used: To find sine or cosine of half an angle from the cosine of the full angle.

In simple words: Gives the half-angle's sine and cosine using the full angle's cosine.

Example: cos²(30°) = (1+cos60°)/2 = 3/4

tan of half angle

tan(A/2) = (1 − cosA) ÷ sinA = sinA ÷ (1 + cosA)

Athe angle

Where it is used: To find the tangent of half an angle — used in integration substitutions.

In simple words: Two equivalent ways to get the tangent of half the angle.

Example: tan45° = (1−cos90°)/sin90° = 1

sin and cos of 15° and 75°

General Solutions

General solution of sinθ = sinα

θ = nπ + (−1)ⁿ α, n = 0, ±1, ±2, …

αa known solution
nany integer

Where it is used: To write all angles that share the same sine — solving trig equations (Class 11).

In simple words: Gives every angle, not just one, that has the same sine value.

Example: sinθ = ½ → θ = nπ + (−1)ⁿ·30°

General solution of cosθ = cosα

θ = 2nπ ± α, n = 0, ±1, ±2, …

αa known solution
nany integer

Where it is used: To list all angles with the same cosine.

In simple words: Every angle with the same cosine as α, going round and round.

Example: cosθ = ½ → θ = 2nπ ± 60°

General solution of tanθ = tanα

θ = nπ + α, n = 0, ±1, ±2, …

αa known solution
nany integer

Where it is used: To list all angles with the same tangent.

In simple words: Tangent repeats every half turn, so add any multiple of 180° to a solution.

Example: tanθ = 1 → θ = nπ + 45°

General solution of sinθ = 0, cosθ = 0

sinθ=0 → θ=nπ; cosθ=0 → θ=(2n+1)π/2

nany integer

Where it is used: The base cases for solving trig equations equal to zero.

In simple words: Sine is zero at whole multiples of 180°; cosine is zero at odd multiples of 90°.

Example: sinθ=0 → θ = 0°, 180°, 360°, …
