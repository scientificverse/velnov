Triangle

Angle sum of a triangle Class 7

∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180°

∠A, ∠B, ∠Cthe three interior angles

Where it is used: To find a missing angle of a triangle when the other two are known — used throughout geometry.

In simple words: The three corners of any triangle always add up to 180 degrees.

Example: If two angles are 60° and 70°, the third = 180−130 = 50°

Exterior angle of a triangle

Exterior angle = sum of the two opposite interior angles

—the outside angle at a corner

Where it is used: To find an exterior angle of a triangle without knowing the third interior angle.

In simple words: The angle formed outside a corner equals the two inside corners far from it, added together.

Example: Interior 50° and 60° → exterior = 110°

Congruence rules

SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS

Sside
Aangle
RHSright angle-hypotenuse-side

Where it is used: To prove two triangles are exactly equal in shape and size.

In simple words: If certain matching sides and angles are equal, the triangles are identical.

Example: Two sides and the angle between them equal → SAS

Similarity and area ratio

Ratio of areas = (Ratio of sides)²

—for two similar triangles

Where it is used: To compare areas of similar figures from their side ratio — maps, scale models.

In simple words: If a shape is scaled by 2, its area grows by 2² = 4 times.

Example: Sides in 2:3 → areas in 4:9

Basic proportionality (Thales)

A line parallel to one side divides the other two sides proportionally

—line parallel to a side of a triangle

Where it is used: To find unknown lengths when a line is drawn parallel to a triangle's side.

In simple words: A line parallel to one side cuts the other two sides in the same ratio.

Example: AD/DB = AE/EC

Polygon

Sum of interior angles of a polygon Class 8

Sum = (n − 2) × 180°

nnumber of sides

Where it is used: To find the total of all inside angles of any polygon — tiling, design, construction.

In simple words: Split the shape into triangles: a shape with n sides makes (n−2) triangles, each 180°.

Example: Pentagon (n=5): (5−2)×180 = 540°

Number of diagonals of a polygon

Diagonals = n(n − 3) ÷ 2

nnumber of sides

Where it is used: To count the diagonals of any polygon.

In simple words: A quick count of how many corner-to-corner lines a shape has.

Example: Hexagon (n=6) → 6×3/2 = 9 diagonals

Pythagoras

Pythagoras theorem Class 9

a² + b² = c²

chypotenuse (longest side, opposite right angle)
a, bthe two shorter sides

Where it is used: In a right-angled triangle, to find one side from the other two — construction, navigation, distances.

In simple words: In an 'L'-shaped corner, the slanted side squared equals the two straight sides squared and added.

Example: a=3, b=4 → c = √(9+16) = 5

Circle

Angle in a semicircle Class 9

∠ in semicircle = 90°

∠angle at the circle from a diameter

Where it is used: A circle theorem used to prove right angles and solve geometry problems.

In simple words: Any angle drawn from the ends of a diameter to a point on the circle is a perfect right angle.

Example: A triangle drawn on a diameter always has a 90° corner.

Angle at centre vs at circumference

Angle at centre = 2 × angle at the circumference

—angles standing on the same arc

Where it is used: A circle theorem used to find unknown angles in circle geometry.

In simple words: An angle made at the centre is always double the same angle made at the edge, on the same arc.

Example: Angle at edge 40° → angle at centre 80°

Tangent and radius

A tangent is perpendicular (90°) to the radius at the point of contact

—tangent touching the circle

Where it is used: A circle theorem used to find angles and lengths involving tangents.

In simple words: Where a line just touches a circle, it meets the radius at a right angle.

Example: Radius to the touch point makes 90° with the tangent

Tangents from an external point

Two tangents from an outside point are equal in length

—tangents from one external point

Where it is used: To find tangent lengths and prove equalities in circle geometry.

In simple words: From a point outside a circle, both touching lines are the same length.

Example: PA = PB for tangents from P

Cyclic quadrilateral

Opposite angles add to 180°

—a four-sided figure inscribed in a circle

Where it is used: To find angles of a quadrilateral whose corners lie on a circle.

In simple words: In a four-sided shape drawn inside a circle, opposite corners add to 180°.

Example: If one angle is 110°, the opposite is 70°

Coordinate Geometry

Distance between two points Class 10

d = √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²)

(x₁, y₁)first point
(x₂, y₂)second point

Where it is used: To find the straight-line distance between two points on a graph — maps, navigation, physics.

In simple words: It's Pythagoras on a graph: the gap sideways and the gap up, squared, added and rooted.

Example: (0,0) to (3,4): d = √(9+16) = 5

Midpoint of a line segment Class 10

M = ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2)

(x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂)the two end points

Where it is used: To find the exact centre point between two points — used in geometry and graphics.

In simple words: Average the two x-values and the two y-values to land exactly in the middle.

Example: Midpoint of (2,4) and (6,8) = (4,6)

Section formula Class 10

P = ((mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂+ny₁)/(m+n))

m : nthe dividing ratio
(x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂)the two points

Where it is used: To find a point that divides a line segment in a given ratio — used in geometry and computer graphics.

In simple words: It finds a point that splits the line between two points in a chosen ratio, not just the middle.

Example: (2,3),(8,9) in 1:2 → P = (4,5)

Slope (gradient) of a line Class 10

m = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)

(x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂)two points on the line
mthe slope

Where it is used: To measure how steep a line is — graphs, ramps, rates of change in science and economics.

In simple words: It's the rise divided by the run: how much the line goes up for every step across.

Example: (1,2) to (3,6): m = (6−2)/(3−1) = 2

Area of a triangle (coordinates) Class 11

A = ½ |x₁(y₂−y₃) + x₂(y₃−y₁) + x₃(y₁−y₂)|

(x₁,y₁),(x₂,y₂),(x₃,y₃)the three vertices

Where it is used: To find the area of a triangle from the coordinates of its corners — surveying, graphics.

In simple words: Plug the three corner points into this pattern to get the area, no height needed.

Example: (0,0),(4,0),(0,3): A = ½|0+12+0| = 6

Centroid of a triangle

G = ((x₁+x₂+x₃)/3, (y₁+y₂+y₃)/3)

(x₁,y₁),(x₂,y₂),(x₃,y₃)the three vertices

Where it is used: To find the balancing point (centroid) of a triangle from its corner coordinates.

In simple words: Average the three corner points to find the triangle's balance point.

Example: (0,0),(6,0),(0,6) → G = (2,2)

Collinearity of three points

Points are collinear if the triangle area = 0

—three points on a graph

Where it is used: To check whether three points lie on the same straight line.

In simple words: If the 'triangle' they form has zero area, the three points are in a line.

Example: (1,1),(2,2),(3,3) give area 0 → collinear

Straight Line

Slope-intercept form of a line Class 11

y = mx + c

mslope (steepness)
cy-intercept (where it crosses the y-axis)

Where it is used: The standard equation of a straight line — graphing, linear relationships, trend lines.

In simple words: 'm' tells how steep the line is and 'c' tells where it starts on the up-axis.

Example: y = 2x + 1 has slope 2 and crosses y-axis at 1

Line through two points (slope form)

y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)

mslope
(x₁,y₁)a known point on the line

Where it is used: To write the equation of a line when a point on it and its slope are known.

In simple words: Start from a known point and use the slope to describe every other point on the line.

Example: Point (1,2), slope 3 → y − 2 = 3(x − 1)

Angle between two lines

tan θ = |(m₁ − m₂) ÷ (1 + m₁m₂)|

m₁, m₂the slopes of the two lines

Where it is used: To find the angle at which two lines cross.

In simple words: Uses the two slopes to find the crossing angle.

Example: m₁=1, m₂=0 → tanθ = 1, so θ = 45°

Perpendicular and parallel lines

Parallel: m₁ = m₂; Perpendicular: m₁ × m₂ = −1

m₁, m₂the slopes

Where it is used: To test if two lines are parallel or at right angles from their slopes.

In simple words: Equal slopes mean parallel; slopes multiplying to −1 mean a right angle.

Example: Slopes 2 and −½ → perpendicular

Distance of a point from a line

d = |ax₁ + by₁ + c| ÷ √(a² + b²)

ax+by+c=0the line
(x₁, y₁)the point

Where it is used: To find the shortest (perpendicular) distance from a point to a line.

In simple words: Gives the straight-line gap between a point and a line.

Example: Point (0,0) to 3x+4y−10=0 → 10/5 = 2

Intercept form of a line

x/a + y/b = 1

ax-intercept
by-intercept

Where it is used: To write a line's equation from where it cuts the two axes.

In simple words: Uses the two points where the line crosses the axes.

Example: Cuts axes at 2 and 3 → x/2 + y/3 = 1

Angles

Complementary and supplementary angles

Complementary: A + B = 90° · Supplementary: A + B = 180°

A, Bthe two angles

Where it is used: To find a missing angle that pairs with another to make a right angle or a straight line.

In simple words: Two angles that add to 90° are complementary; two that add to 180° are supplementary.

Example: Complement of 30° = 60°; supplement of 30° = 150°

Angles on a straight line

Angles on a line add to 180°

—adjacent angles on a straight line

Where it is used: To find a missing angle that sits on a straight line with another.

In simple words: A straight line makes a half turn, so angles along it add to 180°.

Example: If one angle is 120°, the other is 60°

Angles at a point

Angles around a point add to 360°

—angles meeting at one point

Where it is used: To find a missing angle among several meeting at a point.

In simple words: A full turn around a point is 360°, so all the angles there add to 360°.

Example: Three angles 100°+150°+? = 360° → ? = 110°

Vertically opposite angles

Vertically opposite angles are equal

—angles opposite each other where two lines cross

Where it is used: To find equal angles formed when two lines cross.

In simple words: When two lines cross, the angles directly across from each other are equal.

Example: One angle 70° → its opposite is also 70°

Parallel Lines

Angles with parallel lines

Corresponding = equal, Alternate = equal, Co-interior add to 180°

—angles made by a line crossing two parallel lines

Where it is used: To find angles when a transversal cuts two parallel lines.

In simple words: Parallel lines make matching angles equal and same-side inside angles add to 180°.

Example: Alternate angles are always equal
