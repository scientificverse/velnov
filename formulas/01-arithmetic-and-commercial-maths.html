Percentage

Percentage of a number Class 5

Value = (Percentage ÷ 100) × Number

%the percentage (per hundred)
Numberthe total amount

Where it is used: To find a part of a total when the part is given as a percentage — used in discounts, marks, tax, tips and statistics.

In simple words: 'Per cent' means 'out of 100'. To find 20% of something, split it into 100 equal parts and take 20 of them.

Example: 20% of 50 = (20 ÷ 100) × 50 = 10

Convert a fraction to a percentage Class 6

Percentage = Fraction × 100

Fractiona part written as a/b

Where it is used: To express a fraction or a score out of a total as a percentage, so different quantities can be compared easily.

In simple words: Multiply the fraction by 100 to see how much it would be out of 100.

Example: 3/4 = 3/4 × 100 = 75%

One quantity as a percentage of another Class 6

Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100

Partthe amount you have
Wholethe total amount

Where it is used: To find what percentage one number is of another — such as marks obtained out of total marks, or attendance.

In simple words: Divide the part by the whole, then multiply by 100 to turn it into a percentage.

Example: 45 marks out of 60 = (45 ÷ 60) × 100 = 75%

Percentage increase Class 8

Increase % = (Increase ÷ Original) × 100

Increasenew value − old value
Originalthe starting value

Where it is used: To measure growth as a percentage — price rise, population growth, salary hike.

In simple words: It shows how much bigger something got, compared to what it was, out of 100.

Example: Price ₹200 → ₹250: increase 50, so 50/200 × 100 = 25%

Percentage decrease Class 8

Decrease % = (Decrease ÷ Original) × 100

Decreaseold value − new value
Originalthe starting value

Where it is used: To measure a fall as a percentage — price drop, weight loss, reduction in pollution.

In simple words: It shows how much smaller something got, compared to what it was, out of 100.

Example: Price ₹200 → ₹150: decrease 50, so 50/200 × 100 = 25%

Fraction/decimal to percentage

Percentage = Decimal × 100

Decimalthe number in decimal form

Where it is used: To turn a decimal or fraction into a percentage for easy comparison.

In simple words: Move the decimal point two places right (multiply by 100) to get the percentage.

Example: 0.25 = 0.25 × 100 = 25%

Successive discounts (net)

Net = 100 − [x + y − (xy ÷ 100)] % off

x, ythe two discount percents

Where it is used: To find the single equivalent discount when two discounts are applied one after another.

In simple words: Two discounts don't just add — the second acts on the reduced price, so this gives the real total off.

Example: 20% then 10%: 20+10−2 = 28% off

Percentage error

Error % = (|Error| ÷ Actual value) × 100

Errormeasured − actual
Actualtrue value

Where it is used: To express how far a measurement is from the true value, as a percentage.

In simple words: How wrong a measurement is compared to the correct value, out of 100.

Example: Measured 52 vs actual 50 → 2/50×100 = 4%

Net effect of increase then decrease

Net % = a − b − (ab ÷ 100)

aincrease %
bdecrease %

Where it is used: To find the overall change when a value rises by a% and then falls by b%.

In simple words: The rise and fall don't simply cancel — this gives the true overall change.

Example: +20% then −20% → 20−20−4 = −4%

If A is x% more than B

B is less than A by [x ÷ (100+x)] × 100 %

xthe percent A is more than B

Where it is used: To convert 'A is x% more than B' into 'B is how much % less than A'.

In simple words: The percent more one way is not the same as the percent less the other way.

Example: A is 25% more than B → B is 20% less than A

Ratio to percentage

Percentage = (Ratio value) × 100

Ratio valuethe fraction form of the ratio

Where it is used: To convert a ratio into a percentage for comparison.

In simple words: Turn the ratio into a fraction, then multiply by 100.

Example: 3:5 → 3/8 × 100 = 37.5%

Passing marks / exam percentage

Percentage = (Marks obtained ÷ Total marks) × 100

Marks obtainedyour score
Total marksmaximum

Where it is used: To find an exam percentage — the most common everyday use of percentage.

In simple words: Divide your marks by the total and multiply by 100.

Example: 425 out of 500 → 85%

Profit & Loss

Profit Class 7

Profit = Selling Price − Cost Price

SPselling price (price you sell at)
CPcost price (price you bought at)

Where it is used: Used in business and shopkeeping to find the gain made when something is sold for more than it cost.

In simple words: If you sell a thing for more than you paid, the extra money is your profit.

Example: Bought at ₹80, sold at ₹100 → Profit = 100 − 80 = ₹20

Loss Class 7

Loss = Cost Price − Selling Price

CPcost price (price you bought at)
SPselling price (price you sell at)

Where it is used: Used in trade to find how much money is lost when something is sold for less than it cost.

In simple words: If you sell a thing for less than you paid, the money you fall short by is your loss.

Example: Bought at ₹100, sold at ₹80 → Loss = 100 − 80 = ₹20

Profit percentage Class 7

Profit % = (Profit ÷ Cost Price) × 100

ProfitSP − CP
CPcost price

Where it is used: To compare how good a deal is, as a percentage of the cost — the standard way businesses state their gain.

In simple words: It tells you your gain compared to what you spent, out of 100. Higher percent = better deal.

Example: Profit ₹20 on CP ₹80 → 20/80 × 100 = 25%

Loss percentage Class 7

Loss % = (Loss ÷ Cost Price) × 100

LossCP − SP
CPcost price

Where it is used: To state a loss as a percentage of the cost price, so losses on different items can be compared.

In simple words: It shows how big your loss is compared to what you paid, out of 100.

Example: Loss ₹20 on CP ₹100 → 20/100 × 100 = 20%

Selling price from profit % Class 8

SP = CP × (100 + Profit %) ÷ 100

CPcost price
Profit %gain percent

Where it is used: To work out the price to sell at so as to make a required percentage of profit.

In simple words: Add the profit percent to 100, then take that percent of the cost price to get the selling price.

Example: CP ₹200, profit 10% → SP = 200 × 110/100 = ₹220

Cost price from profit %

CP = (SP × 100) ÷ (100 + Profit %)

SPselling price
Profit %gain percent

Where it is used: To work backwards to the cost price when the selling price and profit percent are known.

In simple words: Given the selling price and the profit percent, this recovers what it originally cost.

Example: SP ₹220 at 10% profit → CP = 22000/110 = ₹200

Overall gain/loss on two equal sales

If sold at same price, one at +x% and one at −x%, net = −(x²/100)%

xthe percent

Where it is used: A classic result: selling two items at the same price with equal profit and loss percents gives an overall loss.

In simple words: Equal profit and loss percents don't cancel — you always end with a small loss.

Example: x=10 → net loss = 100/100 = 1%

Selling price with profit and discount

SP = CP × (100+P%)/100; MP set so that after discount SP is reached

CPcost price
P%desired profit

Where it is used: To fix a marked price that still gives the wanted profit after a discount is offered.

In simple words: Shopkeepers mark the price high so that even after a discount they still make their profit.

Example: CP ₹100, want 20% profit after 20% discount → mark ₹150

Dishonest dealer's gain

Gain % = (Error ÷ (True − Error)) × 100

Truetrue weight (e.g. 1000 g)
Errorshortfall in weight

Where it is used: To find the extra profit a trader makes by giving less than the true weight.

In simple words: If a shopkeeper's '1 kg' is actually less, this is the hidden profit they gain.

Example: Sells 900 g as 1 kg → 100/900 × 100 ≈ 11.1%

Discount

Discount Class 8

Discount = Marked Price − Selling Price

MPmarked price (label price)
SPselling price (price actually paid)

Where it is used: Used in shops and sales to find the reduction given on the labelled price.

In simple words: The money taken off the printed price during a sale is the discount.

Example: MP ₹500, SP ₹400 → Discount = ₹100

Discount percentage Class 8

Discount % = (Discount ÷ Marked Price) × 100

DiscountMP − SP
MPmarked price

Where it is used: To express a sale reduction as a percentage of the marked price — how sales are advertised ('20% off').

In simple words: It shows how much is taken off, out of every 100 rupees of the printed price.

Example: Discount ₹100 on MP ₹500 → 100/500 × 100 = 20%

Selling price after discount

SP = MP × (100 − Discount %) ÷ 100

MPmarked price
Discount %percent off

Where it is used: To find the price you actually pay after a percentage discount is applied.

In simple words: Take the printed price, knock off the discount percent, and that's what you pay.

Example: MP ₹500, 20% off → SP = 500 × 80/100 = ₹400

Marked price from selling price

MP = (SP × 100) ÷ (100 − Discount %)

SPselling price
Discount %percent off

Where it is used: To recover the marked (label) price when the paid price and discount are known.

In simple words: Works backwards from the paid price and discount to the printed price.

Example: SP ₹400, 20% off → MP = 40000/80 = ₹500

Simple Interest

Simple Interest Class 7

SI = (P × R × T) ÷ 100

Pprincipal (money borrowed or saved)
Rrate of interest per year (%)
Ttime in years

Where it is used: To find the extra money (interest) paid or earned on a loan or deposit, when interest is the same each year.

In simple words: It's the rent you pay for borrowing money. Bigger loan, higher rate, or longer time all make it more.

Example: P=₹1000, R=5%, T=2 yrs → SI = 1000×5×2/100 = ₹100

Amount (with simple interest) Class 7

A = P + SI

Atotal amount to be paid back
Pprincipal
SIsimple interest

Where it is used: To find the total money returned at the end of a loan or the maturity value of a deposit.

In simple words: Add the interest to the money you started with to get the total you'll have or owe.

Example: P=₹1000, SI=₹100 → A = 1000 + 100 = ₹1100

Rate of simple interest

R = (SI × 100) ÷ (P × T)

SIinterest
Pprincipal
Ttime (years)

Where it is used: To find the yearly interest rate when the interest, principal and time are known.

In simple words: Rearranges the simple-interest formula to solve for the rate.

Example: SI=100, P=1000, T=2 → R = 5%

Time (simple interest)

T = (SI × 100) ÷ (P × R)

SIinterest
Pprincipal
Rrate (%)

Where it is used: To find how long money was invested/borrowed from the interest, principal and rate.

In simple words: Rearranges simple interest to solve for the time.

Example: SI=100, P=1000, R=5 → T = 2 years

Time to double at simple interest

T = 100 ÷ R

Rrate per year (%)

Where it is used: To find how many years it takes money to double under simple interest.

In simple words: Divide 100 by the rate to see how long the money takes to double.

Example: R = 5% → T = 100/5 = 20 years

Principal from simple interest

P = (SI × 100) ÷ (R × T)

SIinterest
Rrate (%)
Ttime (years)

Where it is used: To find the original sum when the interest, rate and time are known.

In simple words: Rearranges the simple-interest formula to find the starting money.

Example: SI=100, R=5, T=2 → P = 10000/10 = ₹1000

Compound Interest

Amount (compound interest) Class 8

A = P × (1 + R/100)^T

Afinal amount
Pprincipal
Rrate per year (%)
Ttime in years

Where it is used: For loans and savings where interest is added to the principal each year, so you earn interest on interest.

In simple words: Each year the interest joins your money, and next year you earn interest on that bigger amount too — it grows faster and faster.

Example: P=₹1000, R=10%, T=2 → A = 1000×(1.1)² = ₹1210

Compound Interest Class 8

CI = A − P

Afinal amount
Pprincipal

Where it is used: To find just the interest part when interest is compounded, by removing the original money.

In simple words: Take away the money you started with from the final amount to see how much extra you gained.

Example: A=₹1210, P=₹1000 → CI = ₹210

CI compounded half-yearly

A = P × (1 + R/200)^(2T)

Pprincipal
Rannual rate (%)
Ttime in years

Where it is used: For deposits/loans where interest is added twice a year instead of once.

In simple words: When interest is added every 6 months, halve the rate and double the number of periods.

Example: P=₹1000, R=10%, T=1 → A = 1000×(1.05)² = ₹1102.50

Difference between CI and SI (2 years)

CI − SI = P × (R ÷ 100)²

Pprincipal
Rrate (%)

Where it is used: A quick way to find how much more compound interest gives than simple interest over 2 years.

In simple words: Over two years, the extra that compounding adds is just P times (R/100) squared.

Example: P=1000, R=10% → CI−SI = 1000×0.01 = ₹10

Rule of 72 (doubling time)

Years to double ≈ 72 ÷ R

Rannual compound rate (%)

Where it is used: A quick estimate of how long money takes to double under compound interest.

In simple words: Divide 72 by the interest rate for a fast estimate of the doubling time.

Example: R = 8% → about 72/8 = 9 years

CI compounded quarterly

A = P × (1 + R/400)^(4T)

Pprincipal
Rannual rate (%)
Tyears

Where it is used: For interest added four times a year (every 3 months).

In simple words: Quarter the rate and quadruple the periods when interest is added every three months.

Example: P=1000, R=8%, T=1 → 1000×(1.02)⁴ ≈ ₹1082.4

Ratio & Proportion

Ratio Class 6

Ratio = a : b = a ÷ b

a, bthe two quantities being compared

Where it is used: To compare two quantities of the same kind — mixing, sharing, map scales, recipes.

In simple words: It tells you how many of one thing there are for each of another, like 2 cups of rice for every 1 cup of dal.

Example: 20 boys and 30 girls → ratio 20:30 = 2:3

Proportion Class 6

a : b = c : d ⇒ a × d = b × c

a, b, c, dfour quantities in proportion

Where it is used: To check if two ratios are equal, or to find a missing value when scaling recipes, maps or prices.

In simple words: When two ratios are equal, the cross-products match. This lets you find a missing amount when keeping the same ratio.

Example: 2:3 = 4:6 because 2×6 = 3×4 = 12

Dividing an amount in a ratio

Share = (its ratio part ÷ total parts) × Amount

Amountthe total to divide

Where it is used: To split money or quantity between people in a given ratio.

In simple words: Add the ratio numbers, then give each person their share of the total.

Example: ₹600 in 1:2 → 200 and 400

Direct proportion

y = k × x (y/x is constant)

kthe constant
x, ythe two quantities

Where it is used: When two quantities rise and fall together — cost vs quantity, distance vs time at fixed speed.

In simple words: If one doubles, the other doubles; their ratio stays the same.

Example: 5 pens ₹50 → 8 pens ₹80

Inverse proportion

x × y = constant

x, ythe two quantities

Where it is used: When one quantity rises as the other falls — speed vs time, workers vs days.

In simple words: If one doubles, the other halves; their product stays the same.

Example: 4 workers 6 days → 8 workers 3 days

Average

Average (Mean) Class 6

Average = Sum of values ÷ Number of values

Sumtotal of all the numbers
Numberhow many numbers there are

Where it is used: To find a single typical value for a set of numbers — average marks, runs, temperature or income.

In simple words: Add everything up and share it out equally. It's the 'middle' amount if everyone had the same.

Example: Marks 10, 20, 30 → (10+20+30)/3 = 20

Average speed (whole journey)

Average speed = Total distance ÷ Total time

Total distancesum of all distances
Total timesum of all times

Where it is used: To find the true average speed of a journey with several stages or stops.

In simple words: Add up all the distance and all the time, then divide — don't just average the speeds.

Example: 120 km in 3 h total → 40 km/h

New average after a change

New avg = (Old sum ± change) ÷ New count

Old sumtotal before
New countnumber of items after

Where it is used: To update an average when a value is added, removed or replaced.

In simple words: Adjust the total, then divide by the new number of items.

Example: Avg of 4 nums=10 (sum 40); add 20 → 60/5 = 12

Average of first n natural numbers

Average = (n + 1) ÷ 2

nhow many numbers

Where it is used: A quick average for 1, 2, 3, … up to n.

In simple words: The average of the first n counting numbers is just the middle value.

Example: Average of 1..9 = 10/2 = 5

Speed, Distance, Time

Speed Class 7

Speed = Distance ÷ Time

Distancehow far (m or km)
Timehow long (s or hours)

Where it is used: To find how fast something moves — vehicles, trains, runners — and to plan travel times.

In simple words: It tells you how much distance is covered in one unit of time. Cover more ground in less time and your speed is higher.

Example: 120 km in 2 hours → Speed = 120/2 = 60 km/h

Distance Class 7

Distance = Speed × Time

Speedhow fast
Timehow long

Where it is used: To find how far something travels when its speed and travel time are known.

In simple words: Multiply how fast you go by how long you go for, to find how far you get.

Example: 60 km/h for 3 hours → Distance = 60×3 = 180 km

Time Class 7

Time = Distance ÷ Speed

Distancehow far
Speedhow fast

Where it is used: To find how long a journey takes when the distance and speed are known.

In simple words: Divide the distance by the speed to find how long the trip will take.

Example: 180 km at 60 km/h → Time = 180/60 = 3 hours

Average speed (equal distances)

Average speed = 2xy ÷ (x + y)

xspeed one way
yspeed the return way

Where it is used: To find the average speed for a round trip covering the same distance at two different speeds.

In simple words: You can't just average the two speeds — this formula gives the correct average for equal distances.

Example: x=60, y=40 → 2·60·40/100 = 48 km/h

Unit conversion (km/h to m/s)

m/s = km/h × (5 ÷ 18)

5/18conversion factor

Where it is used: To convert a speed from kilometres per hour to metres per second (and ×18/5 for the reverse).

In simple words: Multiply km/h by 5/18 to get metres per second.

Example: 72 km/h = 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s

Relative speed (same direction)

Relative speed = a − b

afaster speed
bslower speed

Where it is used: To find how fast one moving object gains on another going the same way.

In simple words: When both move the same way, subtract their speeds to see how fast one catches up.

Example: 60 and 40 km/h same way → gap closes at 20 km/h

Relative speed (opposite directions)

Relative speed = a + b

a, bthe two speeds

Where it is used: To find how fast two objects approach each other head-on.

In simple words: When moving toward each other, add their speeds to see how fast the gap shrinks.

Example: 60 and 40 km/h toward each other → 100 km/h

m/s to km/h

km/h = m/s × (18 ÷ 5)

18/5conversion factor

Where it is used: To convert a speed from metres per second to kilometres per hour.

In simple words: Multiply m/s by 18/5 to get km/h.

Example: 20 m/s = 20 × 18/5 = 72 km/h

Tax (GST)

Price including GST

Final Price = Price × (100 + GST %) ÷ 100

Priceprice before tax
GST %tax rate

Where it is used: To find the final bill amount after Goods and Services Tax is added.

In simple words: Add the tax percent on top of the price to get the total you pay at the counter.

Example: Price ₹1000, GST 18% → 1000 × 118/100 = ₹1180

Growth & Depreciation

Population growth / depreciation

Value = P × (1 ± R/100)^T

Pstarting value
Rrate per period (%)
Tnumber of periods
±+ for growth, − for depreciation

Where it is used: To find future population (growth, +) or the falling value of machines/vehicles (depreciation, −).

In simple words: Same idea as compound interest: use plus for things that grow, minus for things that lose value.

Example: Car ₹5,00,000, depreciates 10%/yr, 2 yrs → 5,00,000×(0.9)² = ₹4,05,000

Value n years ago

Past value = Present ÷ (1 + R/100)ⁿ

Rgrowth rate (%)
nyears back

Where it is used: To find a past population or value from the present one and the growth rate.

In simple words: Divide instead of multiply to step backwards in time.

Example: Now 12100 at 10%/yr → 2 yrs ago = 12100/1.21 = 10000

Time & Work

Combined work rate

1/T = 1/a + 1/b

adays A alone takes
bdays B alone takes
Tdays together

Where it is used: To find how long two people/pipes take together when each one's solo time is known.

In simple words: Add how much each does in a day; the total per day tells how fast they finish together.

Example: A=6 days, B=12 days → 1/T = 1/6+1/12 = 1/4, so T = 4 days

Men–days relationship

M₁ × D₁ = M₂ × D₂

Mnumber of workers
Dnumber of days

Where it is used: To find how many workers or days are needed for the same job.

In simple words: More workers means fewer days; the total worker-days stays the same.

Example: 10 men × 6 days = 15 men × 4 days

Work done in given time

Work = Rate × Time

Ratework per unit time
Timetime worked

Where it is used: To find how much of a job is completed in a given time.

In simple words: Multiply how fast you work by how long you work to find how much gets done.

Example: Rate 1/6 per day, 3 days → 3/6 = half the job

Mixtures & Alligation

Rule of alligation

Cheaper : Dearer = (D − Mean) : (Mean − C)

Ccost of cheaper part
Dcost of dearer part
Meancost of the mixture

Where it is used: To find the ratio in which two ingredients at different prices must be mixed for a target price.

In simple words: Balances how much of the cheap and the costly item to mix to hit an average price.

Example: ₹10 and ₹15 to make ₹12 → (15−12):(12−10) = 3:2

Replacement / repeated dilution

Final = Initial × (1 − x/V)ⁿ

Vtotal volume
xamount replaced each time
nnumber of times

Where it is used: To find how much pure liquid remains after repeated removal and replacement with water.

In simple words: Each time you remove some mixture and top up with water, the pure part shrinks by a fixed fraction.

Example: 40 L, remove 4 L twice → 40×(0.9)² = 32.4 L pure

Partnership

Profit sharing in partnership

Profit ratio = (Capital × Time) of each partner

Capitalmoney invested
Timemonths invested

Where it is used: To split business profit fairly among partners by their investment and duration.

In simple words: Each partner's share depends on how much they put in and for how long.

Example: A: ₹5000×12, B: ₹6000×10 → 60000:60000 = 1:1

Trains

Time for a train to pass a pole

Time = Length of train ÷ Speed

Lengthlength of the train
Speedtrain's speed

Where it is used: To find how long a train takes to cross a pole or a standing person.

In simple words: The train only has to move its own length past a pole, so divide length by speed.

Example: 200 m at 20 m/s → 200/20 = 10 s

Time to cross a platform

Time = (Train length + Platform length) ÷ Speed

Speedtrain's speed

Where it is used: To find how long a train takes to fully cross a platform or bridge.

In simple words: The train must cover its own length plus the platform's length.

Example: (200+300) m at 25 m/s → 500/25 = 20 s

Boats & Streams

Downstream and upstream speed

Down = b + s, Up = b − s

bspeed of boat in still water
sspeed of stream

Where it is used: To find a boat's effective speed with or against a river current.

In simple words: Going with the flow adds the current's speed; going against subtracts it.

Example: Boat 10, stream 2 → down 12, up 8 km/h

Pipes & Cisterns

Two pipes filling together

1/T = 1/a + 1/b

a, bhours each pipe alone takes
Thours together

Where it is used: To find how long two pipes take to fill a tank together (same idea as time & work).

In simple words: Add how much each pipe fills per hour to find the combined filling time.

Example: a=6, b=12 → 1/T = 1/4, T = 4 hours

Ages

Ages after / before n years

Future age = present + n, Past age = present − n

nthe number of years

Where it is used: The basis of age word-problems — everyone ages by the same amount over time.

In simple words: Add the years for the future, subtract them for the past — for every person equally.

Example: Now 20 → in 5 years 25, 5 years ago 15

Clocks

Angle of a clock

Angle = |30H − 5.5M| degrees

Hthe hour
Mthe minutes

Where it is used: To find the angle between the hour and minute hands at a given time.

In simple words: A formula that gives the angle between the two clock hands from the time shown.

Example: At 3:00 → |90 − 0| = 90°
