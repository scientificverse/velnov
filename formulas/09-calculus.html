Derivatives

Power rule (derivative) Class 11

d/dx (xⁿ) = n·xⁿ⁻¹

xthe variable
nthe power

Where it is used: To differentiate power functions — the most-used rule for finding rates of change and slopes.

In simple words: To find how fast xⁿ changes, bring the power down in front and lower the power by one.

Example: d/dx(x³) = 3x²

Derivative of a constant Class 12

d/dx (c) = 0

cany constant

Where it is used: A basic differentiation rule — constants don't change, so their rate of change is zero.

In simple words: A number that never changes has zero rate of change.

Example: d/dx(7) = 0

Derivative of sin and cos Class 12

d/dx(sin x) = cos x, d/dx(cos x) = −sin x

xthe angle in radians

Where it is used: Standard derivatives used in physics (waves, oscillations) and engineering.

In simple words: The rate of change of sine is cosine, and of cosine is negative sine.

Example: slope of sin x at x=0 is cos 0 = 1

Product rule Class 12

d/dx(u·v) = u'v + uv'

u, vtwo functions of x
u', v'their derivatives

Where it is used: To differentiate the product of two functions — used throughout calculus.

In simple words: For two things multiplied, differentiate the first (keep the second), plus keep the first (differentiate the second).

Example: d/dx(x·sin x) = sin x + x·cos x

Chain rule Class 12

d/dx f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) · g'(x)

f, gan outer and inner function
f', g'their derivatives

Where it is used: To differentiate a function inside another function — essential for composite functions.

In simple words: Differentiate the outside, keep the inside, then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

Example: d/dx (sin(x²)) = cos(x²) · 2x

Derivative of eˣ and ln x

d/dx(eˣ) = eˣ, d/dx(ln x) = 1/x

eEuler's number ≈ 2.718
xthe variable (x>0 for ln)

Where it is used: Standard derivatives of the exponential and natural-log functions — growth, decay, finance.

In simple words: eˣ is special — its rate of change equals itself; the log function's rate is one-over-x.

Example: slope of ln x at x=2 is 1/2

Sum and constant-multiple rules

d/dx(u ± v) = u' ± v', d/dx(k·u) = k·u'

u, vfunctions
ka constant

Where it is used: To differentiate sums, differences and constant multiples term by term.

In simple words: Differentiate each piece separately; a constant multiplier just tags along.

Example: d/dx(3x² + 5x) = 6x + 5

Quotient rule

d/dx(u/v) = (u'v − uv') ÷ v²

u, vtwo functions
u', v'their derivatives

Where it is used: To differentiate one function divided by another.

In simple words: For a fraction of functions: bottom times derivative of top, minus top times derivative of bottom, all over bottom squared.

Example: d/dx(x/sin x) = (sin x − x cos x)/sin²x

Derivative of tan, sec, cot, cosec

tan→sec²x, cot→−cosec²x, sec→secx tanx, cosec→−cosecx cotx

xthe angle in radians

Where it is used: Standard derivatives of the other trig functions, used across calculus.

In simple words: The rate-of-change formulas for tangent, secant, cotangent and cosecant.

Example: d/dx(tan x) = sec²x

Derivative of √x and aˣ

d/dx(√x) = 1/(2√x), d/dx(aˣ) = aˣ ln a

aa positive base
xthe variable

Where it is used: Standard derivatives for roots and general exponentials.

In simple words: Handy derivatives for square root and any base raised to x.

Example: d/dx(2ˣ) = 2ˣ ln2

Derivatives of inverse trig

sin⁻¹x → 1/√(1−x²), tan⁻¹x → 1/(1+x²)

xthe variable

Where it is used: Standard derivatives of inverse trig functions (Class 12).

In simple words: The rate-of-change formulas for inverse sine and inverse tangent.

Example: d/dx(tan⁻¹x) = 1/(1+x²)

Integrals

Power rule (integration) Class 12

∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹ ÷ (n+1) + C, n ≠ −1

xthe variable
nthe power
Cconstant of integration

Where it is used: To integrate power functions — finding areas, totals from rates, and reversing differentiation.

In simple words: Integration undoes differentiation: raise the power by one and divide by the new power.

Example: ∫ x² dx = x³/3 + C

Integral of sin and cos Class 12

∫ sin x dx = −cos x + C, ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C

xthe variable
Cconstant of integration

Where it is used: Standard integrals used in physics and engineering for areas and totals.

In simple words: The reverse of differentiating: integrating sine gives negative cosine, integrating cosine gives sine.

Example: ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C

Integral of 1/x and eˣ

∫ (1/x) dx = ln|x| + C, ∫ eˣ dx = eˣ + C

xthe variable
Cconstant of integration

Where it is used: Standard integrals used constantly in calculus, physics and probability.

In simple words: The reverse of the log and exponential derivatives.

Example: ∫ eˣ dx = eˣ + C

Integral of aˣ

∫ aˣ dx = aˣ ÷ ln a + C

aa positive base ≠ 1
Cconstant

Where it is used: To integrate a general exponential function.

In simple words: The reverse of differentiating aˣ.

Example: ∫ 2ˣ dx = 2ˣ/ln2 + C

Integral of sec²x and cosec²x

∫ sec²x dx = tan x + C, ∫ cosec²x dx = −cot x + C

xthe variable
Cconstant

Where it is used: Standard trig integrals — reverses of the tan and cot derivatives.

In simple words: Integrating sec² gives tan; integrating cosec² gives −cot.

Example: ∫ sec²x dx = tan x + C

Integrals giving inverse trig

∫ dx/√(1−x²) = sin⁻¹x + C, ∫ dx/(1+x²) = tan⁻¹x + C

xthe variable
Cconstant

Where it is used: Standard integrals that produce inverse trig functions.

In simple words: These special fractions integrate to inverse sine and inverse tangent.

Example: ∫ dx/(1+x²) = tan⁻¹x + C

Integral of tan x

∫ tan x dx = ln|sec x| + C

xthe variable
Cconstant

Where it is used: A standard integral of the tangent function.

In simple words: Integrating tangent gives the log of secant.

Example: used in many area and volume problems

Definite Integral

Fundamental theorem (definite integral) Class 12

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

Fan antiderivative of f
a, bthe lower and upper limits

Where it is used: To find the exact area under a curve between two points — physics, probability, economics.

In simple words: Find the antiderivative, then subtract its value at the start from its value at the end.

Example: ∫₀¹ x² dx = 1³/3 − 0 = 1/3

Reversing the limits

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = − ∫ᵇₐ f(x) dx

a, bthe limits

Where it is used: A property of definite integrals used to simplify calculations.

In simple words: Swapping the start and end of an integral just flips its sign.

Example: ∫₀¹ = −∫₁₀

Area between curve and x-axis

Area = ∫ₐᵇ y dx

ythe curve's equation
a, bthe x-limits

Where it is used: To find the area enclosed by a curve and the x-axis between two points.

In simple words: Integrating the curve's height across an interval gives the area underneath.

Example: Area under y=x² from 0 to 2 = 8/3

Limits

Limit of sin x over x Class 11

lim (x→0) (sin x ÷ x) = 1

xangle in radians approaching 0

Where it is used: A fundamental limit used to derive the derivative of sine and in many proofs.

In simple words: As the angle gets tiny, sine of the angle and the angle itself become practically equal.

Example: For very small x, sin x ≈ x

Standard limit (xⁿ − aⁿ)

lim (x→a) (xⁿ − aⁿ)/(x − a) = n·aⁿ⁻¹

nthe power
athe point

Where it is used: A fundamental limit used to derive the power rule of differentiation.

In simple words: This limit is exactly the derivative of xⁿ at x = a.

Example: n=2, a=3 → limit = 6

Exponential and log limits

lim(x→0)(eˣ−1)/x = 1, lim(x→0) log(1+x)/x = 1

xapproaching 0

Where it is used: Standard limits used to differentiate exponential and log functions.

In simple words: For tiny x, eˣ−1 and log(1+x) both behave just like x.

Example: (e^0.001 − 1)/0.001 ≈ 1

Limit giving e

lim (x→∞) (1 + 1/x)ˣ = e

e≈ 2.718
xgrowing large

Where it is used: The famous limit that defines Euler's number e — compound interest, growth.

In simple words: Compounding a 100% return over more and more tiny steps approaches e.

Example: (1 + 1/1000)¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 2.717

Application

Slope of a curve at a point

Slope = dy/dx at that point

dy/dxthe derivative of y with respect to x

Where it is used: To find the steepness of a curve at any point — tangents, maxima and minima, velocity.

In simple words: The derivative gives the exact tilt of the curve at whatever point you plug in.

Example: y = x², dy/dx = 2x → slope at x=3 is 6

Increasing and decreasing functions

f'(x) > 0 → increasing; f'(x) < 0 → decreasing

f'(x)the derivative

Where it is used: To find where a function rises or falls — graphs, optimisation.

In simple words: A positive slope means going up; a negative slope means going down.

Example: f(x)=x²: f'=2x>0 for x>0 → increasing

Maxima and minima test

f'(x) = 0, then f''(x) < 0 → max, f''(x) > 0 → min

f'first derivative
f''second derivative

Where it is used: To find the highest and lowest points of a function — optimisation problems.

In simple words: Flat slope marks a turning point; the second derivative says if it's a peak or a valley.

Example: f(x)=x²: f'=0 at x=0, f''=2>0 → minimum

Rate of change

Rate = dy/dt (derivative with respect to time)

ythe changing quantity
ttime

Where it is used: To find how fast something changes with time — speed, growth, filling rates.

In simple words: The derivative with respect to time tells how quickly a quantity is changing.

Example: If s = t², velocity = ds/dt = 2t
